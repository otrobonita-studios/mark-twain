Subject: Morning Brief, Sat. June 20 — Two truces nobody trusts, a King of the North, and a Swedish armada that may yet sail

Jesper,

It is Saturday, the twentieth of June, and the wires are humming the way the telegraph used to hum the night before a hanging — a great deal of urgent clicking and no clear notion of whether the rope will hold. I have been down to the dock of the world this morning and fished out four fish for you. Two are foreign, caught on the Reuters line; two are Swedish, hauled up by Sveriges Radio. None of them are entirely fresh, but in this trade nothing ever is. Here is your breakfast.

----------------------------------------------------------------

1) THE PEACE THAT KEEPS GETTING SHOT AT — Reuters
(US–Iran, Israel–Hezbollah, and the Strait of Hormuz)

A man will sign a treaty in the morning and reload by supper, and that, in short, is the news from the Middle East. Two days back, Wednesday, Mr. Trump and Iran's President Pezeshkian put their names to a fourteen-point memorandum — signed by wire, like a man too busy to come to his own duel — promising sixty days to argue out a permanent peace, an end to the war, and the reopening of the Strait of Hormuz to honest commerce. By Friday the ink was behaving like most ink: it ran. The technical talks that were to open in a quiet Swiss village were called off, the Swiss themselves confirming the cancellation; Vice President Vance, who was to lead the American delegation, found his trunk suddenly too heavy to lift, the White House blaming "logistics" while Tehran, less delicately, blamed Israel's guns in Lebanon.

And the guns were busy. Israeli strikes Friday killed at least forty-seven souls in southern Lebanon by the Lebanese tally, after Hezbollah killed four Israeli soldiers and a tank. Then, near four in the afternoon Beirut time, the parties agreed — through American and Qatari middlemen, with Iran's quiet blessing, and first reported by Reuters — to a ceasefire, which is the modern word for the pause a man takes to catch his breath before the next swing. Out in the Strait, the ships are creeping back: twenty-five crossed Thursday, the most since April, which is progress the way one swallow is summer. It is a peace held together with spit, suspicion, and a sixty-day egg-timer, and I would not sell my umbrella on the strength of it.

----------------------------------------------------------------

2) THE KING OF THE NORTH KNOCKS ON DOWNING STREET — Reuters
(Andy Burnham wins Makerfield; Starmer digs in)

In Britain they have invented a tidy trick: you may become Prime Minister without troubling the voters at all, provided you trouble the right eighty-one of your colleagues. Andy Burnham, the mayor of Manchester they call the "King of the North," has just got himself the only thing he was missing — a seat in Parliament — by winning a by-election in Makerfield with very near fifty-five percent of the vote, beating Nigel Farage's Reform man by better than nine thousand. The seat was vacated for him on purpose, the previous occupant stepping aside like a gentleman giving up his chair to a lady, and the whole post-industrial parish thus became, for five weeks, the kingmaker of a nation.

Now Mr. Burnham wants Mr. Starmer's job, and Mr. Starmer, whose popularity has gone down the way a steamboat goes down — slowly, then all at once — declines to oblige. "Yes, I will run, I will stand," says the Prime Minister, which is the bravest thing a man can say while his own crew is eyeing the lifeboats. It wants but a fifth of Labour's members to start the fight. I have seen riverboat captains cling to the wheel of a sinking vessel out of pure principle, and I will say this for them: the river did not much care about their principles either. Watch this space; the weekend, they say, will be full of whispering.

----------------------------------------------------------------

3) WILL SWEDEN JOIN THE ARMADA AT HORMUZ? — Sveriges Radio (Ekot)

Here the foreign quarrel comes calling at the Swedish door. The Americans announced an operation to begin this very morning — better than a hundred aircraft and warships, by their own count — to shepherd the merchant ships penned up in the Persian Gulf safely out through Hormuz. Sveriges Radio, with the dry skepticism of a man counting another man's poker chips, reports that the "substance" of the thing is "very unclear," which is the politest way ever devised to say nobody has shown the receipts.

And Sweden? Ekot's midday word is that Sweden may yet take part. The defense minister declines to rule it out, noting the country belongs to a coalition of some forty nations pledged to this work, and the question now perfumes the alliance's gathering of defense ministers in Brussels. So a peaceable northern country that would mostly like to be left alone with its coffee may find itself dispatching ships to the hottest, narrowest stretch of saltwater on the globe — all to guarantee that the oil keeps flowing for everyone who swore last week the war was over. The nineteenth century sent gunboats to keep the trade lanes open and called it civilization; the twenty-first sends a committee from Brussels and calls it a coalition. The water is the same.

----------------------------------------------------------------

4) THE MODERATES PROMISE YOU A THOUSAND KRONOR — Sveriges Radio (Ekot)

With a September election bearing down like weather, the Moderates have reached into the oldest hat in politics and pulled out the oldest rabbit: a tax cut. At a morning press conference, finance minister Elisabeth Svantesson unveiled the party's pledge — a strengthened job-tax deduction worth up to a thousand kronor a month for everyone who works, the favor extended to those who have worked and since retired, with lower tax promised on pension that was earned by honest labor. For an ordinary family with children, they reckon, it could mean two thousand kronor more in the pocket each month.

The bill comes to some forty-four billion kronor, to be paid — naturally — by trimming foreign aid, bureaucracy, and benefits, which is to say by the three groups least likely to be standing in the room when the promise is made. I have never yet met the candidate who proposed to fund his generosity out of his own hide, and I have stopped waiting for him. Still, a thousand kronor is a thousand kronor, and a voter, like a trout, is most reliably caught with something shiny he can see and a hook he cannot.

----------------------------------------------------------------

That is the morning's haul, Jesper. Two ceasefires that flinch at loud noises, one mayor measuring the curtains at Number Ten, a Swedish fleet that may sail to a quarrel it did not start, and a tax cut dangling like bait over an election-year pond. Same human circus as in my day — only the tent is bigger and the wire faster.

Yours in print and provocation,
"Mark"
