There are no
 fewer than 10 e-readers in my house at any given time, and I wouldn't have it any other way.
While nothing replaces the feel of a physical book, I usually reach for one of my e-readers as part of my nighttime routine. To me, these tablets are the easiest way to read because they grant immediate access to whatever is in my TBR pile at a given time. Whether a 
library book through the Libby app
 or the series I'm buying up because I can't wait any longer for a copy to be available, whatever I want to read, I can access it immediately on my e-reader.
It’s also convenient that e-readers (short for “ebook readers”) can store thousands of books at a time. These slim devices use E Ink screens to make the digital pages feel like a paper book to your eye in terms of the reading experience. They’re also designed to strain your eyes less than other digital devices, like your phone or computer screen.
And while many strongly associate 
Kindle
 with e-reader, you don't have to turn to Amazon for the best device. In fact, my all-around favorite isn't a Kindle at all. Read on to learn the best e-readers that I, along with other WIRED reviewers, have tried.
Updated August 2026: I’ve added the Kindle Colorsoft Kids, Boox Go 6 (Gen II), and Xteink X4 Pro as new picks in this guide. I’ve also ensured all links and prices are up to date.

If you're looking for an e-reader that has everything from a color screen and page-turner buttons to the ability to double as a digital notebook, then the 
Kobo Libra Colour is the one

Not only does the Libra Colour pack a well-priced color screen, but its 7-inch screen also packs an adjustable front light and dark mode, and it can rotate to landscape mode for reading. It's also made from recycled materials, is waterproof (rated

This means you have to search for your reads on the Kobo e-reader.
The Libra Colour is also 
repairable
, though I haven't come across any component that needs fixing yet. Kobo also launched its own external page-turner called the 
Kobo Remote ($30)

Kobo increased the price of its e-readers in May 2026, including the Libra Colour, which rose $30 to $260. Even so, Kobo often discounts its e-readers during major sale events.

The best Amazon e-reader is hands-down the 
Kindle Paperwhite
 (8/10, WIRED Recommends). Of all

If you're shopping for a Kindle, it's worth remembering that the best time to get a deal on a Kindle is during sale events like Amazon Prime Day or other similar holiday sales.

Technically, any great e-reader will work just as well for kids as it will for adults. But the Kindle Kids line lets you create an account that considers their age. Kindle’s Amazon Kids+ membership (as opposed to a Kindle Unlimited membership) ensures little ones only have access to age-appropriate content.
The Kindle Kids line includes a few options, like the 
Kindle Kids ($130)
 and 
Kindle Paperwhite Kids ($180)
, both of which were updated in 2024 with black-and-white screens. But my favorite Kindle for kids is the 
Kindle Colorsoft Kids
 (8/10, WIRED Recommends), which came out last year. This edition not only comes with a bright color screen (though its display is more muted than a true colorful book), but with a full year of free Kids+ (which starts at $8 per month) instead of the six months that the non-color versions offer. You'll get included covers with fun illustrated covers, too, but that's true for all versions of the Kindle Kids.
I like the Colorsoft Kids model better than the main Colorsoft device for adults. That’s mainly because kids’ books tend to be more suited to make use of the color capability. Sure, it's pretty seeing my book covers in colorful glory, but within the pages, I usually don't see the color features in action. Kids’ books, however, make great use of color features—especially if your little children like graphic novels. The Colorsoft version only comes in the larger 7-inch screen, so if your kid would prefer a smaller option, you'll need to choose the base Kindle Kids model.

In this day and age, buying a book (or any other digital content) doesn't always feel like truly owning it. Your purchased book is usually locked into a certain device or application, like the Kindle app, for example. But you can own raw files of books, usually in electronic publication (ePub) format. Not all retailers sell ePub files, but you can get them from sites like 
Standard Ebooks
 and 
eBooks.com
.
Micro e-readers from Xteink make using ePub files easy. These little devices fit easily into my clothing pockets, which is a feat. Their magnet backing also lets them seamlessly connect to the back of your phone, though it’s a little large for the back of my iPhone 16 Pro.
The X4 Pro is the newest in this line of e-readers, and it sits comfortably in my hand. It can display about a paragraph and a half at a time on its crisp 4.3-inch screen. It pulls in images, too, though they appear a bit grainy. It has a touchscreen and home button that feels like a crossbreed of a Kindle and an older iPhone. It also has a warm, adjustable front light you won't find on Xteink's other e-readers.
It works with the easy-to-use Xteink app: Either use Wi-Fi to send book files from your phone or transfer files via a USB-C cord. The main hiccup I encountered while testing is that any media you upload needs to be DRM-free, meaning free of digital rights management software that can restrict access. Standard Ebooks are always DRM-free, and eBooks.com has a 
DRM-free section
 you can shop.
Ultimately, it's a fun, tiny e-reader, but you need to be file savvy to use it most effectively. You can also customize it with other software, like CrossPoint, if that's up your alley.

Boox Go 7 ($270)
 and

 Boox is known for its wide range of e-paper tablets, including the Boox Go 6 (Gen II) I recommend above. If you're looking for something a little larger with a similar Android-based experience, the Go 7 and Go Color 7 are both solid choices. There are page-turner buttons, and it's a nice square shape that's easy to hold and use. If you, like me, don't use color features a ton, it's worth saving the extra money on the black-and-white model. Regardless, it's great that the price difference between the two models isn't steep.
Boox Palma 2 for $316

 This is a fun, pocket-sized device, with fingerprint recognition, a faster CPU, and a newer version of Android than previous models. Former WIRED reviewer Adrienne So loved using it with the Kindle app, but she wishes it cost less. It's currently unavailable and has been for a while; I'm not sure it'll come back in stock, but the 
Boox Palma 2 Pro ($400)

Kindle Colorsoft Signature for $280
 and 
Kindle Colorsoft ($250)
:
 The 
Kindle Colorsoft Signature
 (7/10, WIRED Recommends) is Amazon's first Kindle with a color screen. It has a screen with an oxide backplane, which Amazon says delivers strong contrast and image quality. It also has custom-formulated coatings and nitride LEDs for brightness and color accuracy. But the screen is capped at 150 ppi for color images, versus 300 ppi for black-and-white, making the reading experience a little fuzzier than on the latest Paperwhite. Processing color images takes much longer than black-and-white ones on the Paperwhite, and the Colorsoft is missing features that are basically standard on other color e-readers, like page-turn buttons and stylus support. It's not a

Kindle Scribe (2nd Gen, 2024) for $400
: The second-generation 
Kindle Scribe
 (8/10, WIRED Recommends) is a great option if you want an e-reader that doubles as a digital notebook while staying within the Kindle ecosystem. It has a 10.2-inch (300 ppi) paper-like display with features like an adjustable warm light and an auto-adjusting front light. The included Premium Pen also has a soft-tipped eraser (mimicking that of a No.2 pencil). With the Active Canvas feature and expandable margins, you can also mark up ebooks and write notes in the side panel. Amazon has launched three new Kindle Scribe models since this one, but this older edition remains my favorite Kindle e-reader with digital-notebook capabilities.
Kindle Scribe (3rd Gen, 2025) for $500

 The latest 
Kindle Scribe

It has a new homepage design that simplifies accessing your latest books and notebooks, plus a quick-notes capability that lets you pin a note to your homepage for easy note-taking. It also has a new set of AI features that can provide summaries and even send your notes to an Alexa+-powered speaker so you can ask questions about them. Overall, though, these features aren't necessary and just drive up the price. The older 
Kindle Scribe (2nd Gen)

FAQs
How Do You Get Ebooks?
AccordionItemContainerButton
Ebooks can be cheap, but as with regular books, the cost can add up if you're a big reader. To keep costs low, take advantage of your library card and check out ebooks instantly from the comfort of your home. We have a detailed guide that explains 
How to Get Free Kindle Books With Your Library Card
, but the short version is that most libraries distribute books via 
OverDrive
. You can access these books a few ways: through Kindle or directly from OverDrive with a Kobo, or you can download the 
Libby
 app. These ebooks are automatically removed from your device and returned to the library when they're due.
Each e-reader's own book service varies in quality. My favorite is 
Kindle Unlimited for $12 a month
, which includes millions of titles, including audiobooks and magazines. Amazon's 
Prime Reading
 also offers a handful of free books every month, which is good to know if you already have a 
Prime account
. You can even lend books to friends and family for a short while (or have them share a book with you). For more details on each option, check out our guide to the 
Best Ebook Subscription Services
 here.
What File Formats Do E-Readers Support?
AccordionItemContainerButton
The most widely used format is ePub, which works natively with all the e-readers in this guide except for Kindle—
technically
. You can still upload ePub files to your Kindle, but you'll have to use the “Send to Kindle” feature (through Amazon, your email, or the Kindle app) to convert it to Amazon's proprietary format called AZW. (Newer Kindles support AZW3 and KFX, specifically). Other formats that will work with these e-readers include PDF, TXT, HTML, DOC, and DOCX.
Can I Install Apps on an E-Reader?
AccordionItemContainerButton
Kindle, Kobo, and Nook all use proprietary operating systems that don't allow you to install third-party apps. The Boox, on the other hand, runs Android (mainly Android 11 and newer). That means you can use it to download any apps available in the Google Play Store or side-load apps from your computer.
Do You Need an E-Reader? What About a Phone or Tablet?
AccordionItemContainerButton
You don't 
have
 to buy a separate device to read ebooks. Smartphones and tablets are perfect for this purpose. You can use Amazon's Kindle app to read ebooks (
iOS
, 
Android
) and download OverDrive's Libby app (
iOS
, 
Android
) for library-book access. However, the E Ink screens found in e-readers are just nicer to look at. I also like that these devices are one-trick ponies; there are no distracting notifications or social media feeds ripe for 
doomscrolling
.
If you'd still rather have one smart device you can use for reading 
and
 other purposes, take a look at our 
Best Tablets
 and 
Best iPads
 guides for recommendations.
What About a Digital Notebook?
AccordionItemContainerButton
I've tried just about every digital notebook on the market; these devices are designed for handwritten notes on an E Ink screen. E-readers with that capability usually still prioritize reading, though there are plenty of models I like that can do both. The Kobo Libra Colour is my favorite option for annotating books, and it can double as a small digital notebook. If you want a larger option, check out my 
full guide to every digital notebook I've tested
.
How Do I Test E-Readers?
AccordionItemContainerButton
The most important step I take when testing e-readers is to read on them! I spend hours reading books acquired in a variety of formats, including books I purchased directly from each brand's store, downloaded from local libraries via OverDrive/Libby, and PDFs acquired for free from God knows where.
In the process, I evaluate battery life, brightness, performance lag, screen responsiveness, and text sharpness. I check for extra tools, like whether the tablet in question accommodates accessories like a stylus. I also note features like waterproofing, annotation capability, and access to exclusive subscription services.
