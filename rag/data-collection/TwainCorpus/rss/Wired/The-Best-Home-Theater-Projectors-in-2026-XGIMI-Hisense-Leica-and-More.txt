Xgimi
Picking the best projector was not easy, especially since there's so much competition. The Xgimi Titan Noir Max won me over because of the dynamic, self-adjusting iris. As you watch a movie or show, the projector adjusts to allow more light in (for bright scenes) or closes to reduce light (for dark scenes). For example, during a nighttime sequence in the movie 
Hoppers
 on Disney+, the iris will close to make the blacks and grays look dark and richly detailed. That feature alone sets the Titan Noir Max apart.
But it’s also highly capable in many other ways. It has an outstanding 10,000:1 native contrast ratio and a 7,000-lumen brightness rating, which helped it blast through all of my benchmarks and test scenarios. An early morning scene in the movie 
The Creator
 looked exactly like I would expect it to in real life, showing texture on the seaside cliffs. Even in a sun-drenched room, battle scenes in the movie 
War Machine
 looked bright and clear—outperforming the Hisense XR10 model.
More functionally, it has four adjustable feet that allow you to adjust the height or even set the projector on a side table and angle it toward a wall.
One downside is that, like many projectors, it doesn't natively run a streaming OS like Google TV, but I prefer to connect a Blu-ray player or 
Google TV Streamer
 anyway.

I’ll never forget the first time I played the game 
007 First Light
 projected on a wall in my house using the 
Hisense XR10

The big and boxy XR10 is exactly what I would expect a high-end cinema projector to look like. It’s heavy at 23.4 pounds, but that’s because of its massive speaker and lens. Although I prefer the long adjustable legs on the Xgimi Titan Noir Max, the XR10 is stable and has a nicely subdued dark metallic look.

The XR10 is a monster of a home cinema projector, with only the Titan Noir Max performing just a bit better on color and brightness. And you do need to adjust the iris manually in the settings, though (unlike the automatic adjustments of the Titan Noir Max).

I had to double check the price of the 
BenQ W4100i
 because it's so much cheaper than my other top picks. Yet, of all the home cinema projectors I've tested in the last few months, the quick and easy setup on this model, plus a few unusual perks, made it a top pick.
It's easy to configure, even though it uses the older Android TV operating system. I asked the onboard Google Assistant bot to start the install process and it only took a few minutes to do the whole thing. While the dark gray enclosure is understated, I liked how easy it was to adjust the focus and throw distance using analog levers on the top of the projector.
At this price, the W4100i is not meant to compete with the titans of home cinema like the Hisense XR10, and it matches an older color gamut spec (Rec.709). The lumens rating is only 3,200, far below the Titan Noir Max's 7,000.
Still, there are a few interesting perks with the BenQ W4100i. It has a mode that can match the 24p frame rate of movies for a more fluid look. With the refresh rate cranked to 240 Hz, playing the game 
NTE
 on my 
gaming laptop
, I came to realize just how much I like using this projector for low-latency gaming. My controller flicks were rendered immediately during chaotic battle scenes.
I’m not sure why BenQ insists on still using a dongle attachment for the operating system. You can skip it altogether if you use one of the HDMI ports for a Blu-ray player or another device. In the end, the lower-than-expected price, low latency, and easy setup are what sold me on this BenQ.

Frequently Asked Questions
Why Use a Home Theater Projector?
AccordionItemContainerButton
It’s an eternal debate—should you pull the shades, fire up a home theater projector, and watch a movie like you’re at the local cineplex? Or is it better to use a massive OLED or RGB television that will show a clearer image during a sunny day? Here’s my take after testing tons of the latest projectors and TVs: I prefer projectors for movies because I can max out the screen size to 120 inches or more. There’s just nothing like watching a 12-foot projected image of a movie with Dolby Atmos surround sound at home.
What Do You Need to Use a Home Theater Projector?
AccordionItemContainerButton
The short answer is quite simple: You just need a white wall. Purists will say a true home cinema experience requires a projector screen and a windowless room, and I have a testing room just for that purpose. However, all of the models I’m recommending are so bright and colorful that they perform quite well on a large wall without needing a projector screen. I do recommend room-darkening shades, though, because a darker room does create a better image.
Why Are Home Theater Projectors So Expensive?
AccordionItemContainerButton
Consumer-grade home theater projectors cost around $3,000 or more. In my experience, the latest large OLED and RGB televisions can easily cost just as much. I strongly recommend sticking to the $3,000 price point for a home cinema projector, though, because many midrange models (e.g., under $2,000) don’t look that colorful or bright. They also suffer from some serious contrast problems. For serious movie and TV fans, I’d avoid projectors that cost less than $1,000.
