Choosing the best action camera used to be simple: Go with GoPro. That's still good advice, as the GoPro Mission 1 Pro is our top pick, but there are also some competitors worth considering, especially if your needs don't require the more expensive Mission 1 Pro.

The decision is not easy, especially since so many of them look alike, have similar features, and do the same thing—at least, on paper. So, to figure out what exactly makes the best action camera, we dove with them, climbed with them, surfed with them, and handed them to reckless 14-year-olds on bikes. We found several great options that will survive your adventures on the ski slope, at the beach, or at the skate park.

Be sure to also read our guide to the Best 360 Cameras, as there's considerable overlap these days with action cameras. In some cases, a 360 camera might be the better option; most can shoot in a single-lens “action camera mode." We've also got guides to the Best Travel Cameras, Best Mirrorless Cameras, and Best Instax Cameras.

Updated August 2026: I've updated our top pick to GoPro's new Mission 1 Camera. I've also removed the GoPro Hero 12 Black because it's no longer a good value, and I've updated prices and links throughout.

    It costs more, but it also does more. GoPro's Mission 1 Pro is the best action camera on the market, thanks to the large 1-inch sensor and advanced GP3 imaging processor. But specs are not what set the Mission 1 apart so much as the quality of video, which has great natural color and looks more cinematic than any other action camera I've tried.

    There are actually three Mission 1 cameras: the entry-level Mission 1, the slightly better Mission 1 Pro, and the Mission 1 Pro ILS, which uses interchangeable lenses based on the Micro Four Thirds mount (as of this guide update, the ILS isn't available yet). The main difference between the Mission 1 and the Pro model is the higher frame rates available in the Pro. I have not tested the non-Pro model, but if you don't need super-slow-motion footage, you can save $100 by getting the base Mission 1.

    The Mission 1 Pro's video capabilities run up to 8K/60 frames per second, with 4K/240-fps slow motion possible (you can even shoot 1080p at the slightly insane frame rate of 960 fps). Curiously, there is no 5.3K video like the Hero 13 Black has; the 8K fills that role here, albeit at the expense of much larger video files. But even if you have no plans to ever export anything as 8K, the ability to crop when you're editing makes the Mission 1 Pro a more powerful camera than most. Perpetual GoPro haters will be disappointed to learn that Mission 1 Pro also has excellent low-light performance, on par with and sometimes exceeding the competition.

    Osmo Action 6

    This was our overall pick before the Mission 1 cameras showed up, but if those are more than you need (or want to spend), DJI's Osmo Action 6 is the next-best action camera. It can shoot 8K video, though it's not nearly as nice as the 8K coming out of the Mission 1 Pro. While not the best quality available, 8K lets you crop in on details, and you can output a great-looking video when you downscale to 4K or 1080p. You can do that with either the 2X zoom in-camera, or after the fact when you're editing, and you'll still have usable 4K footage when exporting your movies. The high-speed capture is quite good, shooting 4K/120 fps (when shooting in 4:3 mode).

    DJI really improved the default color rendering and contrast in the Action 6, to the point that I find its default color profiles to be fine most of the time. It also supports log recording, should you want to color-grade your footage in an editor. Low-light performance is quite good, as good as the Mission 1 Pro, in fact. That's true whether you use SuperNight mode (designed to optimize low-light footage) or not. I should note, though, that there are no manual controls, but the automatic modes were pretty smart most of the time in my testing.

    DJI has followed GoPro's lead, adding some extra lenses to the Action 6. There's a macro lens and an ultrawide lens (now known as the FOV Boost lens) with a 182-degree field of view. These extra options make the Action 6 more versatile in the field.

    Not everyone needs the 8K powerhouse that is our top pick, the Mission 1 Pro. We have a separate guide to choosing the right GoPro, but for most people, the Hero 13 Black is the best option after the Mission 1 series. Its interchangeable lens system gives the Hero 13 Black more versatility than the true budget GoPros like the Lit Hero.

    The Hero 13 Black with the standard lens is lighter and narrower than the Mission 1 Pro, and still plenty capable, with the ability to shoot 5.3K/60-fps video, 4K/120 fps, and 2.7K/240 fps. The 5.3K 10-bit video out of the GoPro Hero 13 is very nearly the best of the cameras on this page, bested only by the Mission 1 Pro. Where the Hero 13 falters is in low-light performance, which frankly is just not good. I almost never shoot with an action camera in low light, so the fact that Hero 13 sucks at handling it (and it really does) doesn't matter to me.

    The Hero 13 Black added a nice magnetic mounting system, finally catching up to competitors, and it's great. But it seems like everyone forgot that GoPro has always had a quick mount system that is also great and continues to be what I use most. In addition to the standard lens, there is the Ultra Wide Lens Mod with a 177-degree field of view, a Macro Lens Mod for close-ups, and an Anamorphic Lens Mod with a 21:9 aspect ratio for those sweet, sweet movie-style lens flares. All of the extra lenses are automatically detected by the Hero 13 the minute you attach them, and settings are automatically adjusted accordingly. This is quite frankly the best feature of the camera, especially for the ND filters, which are normally quite a pain to set up with the proper shutter speed.

    If you're going to be filming yourself at all, the Insta360 Ace Pro 2 is the camera to get. It has a great lens and sensor co-engineered with Leica, and it can record 8K video at up to 30 fps. The 8K is not great for action—it has quite a few digital artifacts in fast-moving scenes—but as with the other 8K cameras, it's nice for cropping in while retaining 4K video quality. The must-have feature for vlogging is the flip-up rear screen. While other action cameras offer a small front screen to compose selfies, the entire back of the Ace Pro is a screen that flips up, and it's easier to frame yourself. It also makes it easy to shoot from the hip and other unusual angles.

    The video quality is impressive, especially the low-light performance, which is much better than the GoPro Hero 13. The Ace Pro 2 has great battery life and a magnetic mounting system that's different from DJI's, but also excellent. The big downside to the Ace Pro 2 is that it doesn't support 10-bit color, which means you have fewer options to colorize footage in post-production.

    The Ace Pro 2 has added a set of extra lenses like the GoPro Hero 13, as well as a very cool camera grip that makes holding the Ace Pro 2 more like a traditional point-and-shoot camera. There's even an instant printer that attaches to the bottom of the grip and effectively makes your Ace Pro 2 into a Polaroid-style instant camera. These extras a fun way to extend the usefulness of the Ace Pro 2. It still might not replace a “real” camera, but the grip makes it much more comfortable to spend a whole day shooting with the Ace Pro 2.

    DJI

    Osmo Nano

    $389

    Amazon

    $389

    Adorama

    You could argue that DJI is copying Insta360 here, which pioneered the detachable action camera with the Go series, most recently the Go Ultra (see below). But the first thing I thought of when I started testing the DJI Osmo Nano is the DJI Action 2, a weird blip in the action camera world that came out and vanished in barely a year. I was always fond of the Action 2 and its strange, detachable, magnetic configuration. The Osmo Nano feels like DJI took the lessons from the Action 2, borrowed some ideas from Insta360, and out came the Osmo Nano.

    The Osmo Nano consists of the Vision Dock (as DJI calls the screen and base) and the camera portion. They can attach with the screen facing either direction, using the same magnetic mount that DJI uses in other cameras. Crucially, this means the camera pod can be attached via any DJI magnetic mount, something very convenient if you're already heavily invested in the DJI ecosystem. The dock works from up to 33 feet away and offers a bright, 1.96-inch touchscreen. There are two models available, one with 64 GB of built-in memory and one with 128 GB. Both have an SD card slot if you need more storage space. The 128-GB model offers a higher download rate when transferring footage, but it's $30 more.

    Nano uses a 1/1.3-inch sensor (the same one that is in the DJI Action 5), and you get some things here that you do not with the Insta360 Go cameras, including support for 10-bit color, 4K/60 fps video, and full-sensor 4:3 4K video at up to 50 fps. Video out of the Nano looks good. DJI appears to have changed its color science somewhat. Video from the Nano looked more natural and less aggressively sharpened than the company's previous cameras. If you shoot in 10-bit D-Log M (a color profile created by DJI) and do your coloring in post, you'll have about 13 stops of dynamic range to work with. That’s really nice when you're filming difficult scenes like riding a trail through the trees in midday sunlight.

    What I didn't love about the Nano is the design. If you want to film yourself, the Insta360’s flip-up screen is easier to use and works better. The camera pod is also considerably larger than the Go 3S' pod. That said, the video quality here is better in almost every instance I tested, and it's $150 cheaper. The added bonus of integrating well with the rest of DJI's products makes the Osmo Nano a great option.

    If I had to pick a video trend of the year, it'd be solo adventures shot from a third-person point of view—as if you have a film crew following you. The flip side to the poor battery life is that the battery is small, meaning you can carry two or three when you go out.

    The OM System (formerly Olympus) TG-7 isn't a strap-it-to-your-head-and-shred camera, but if you're shooting underwater—a common use case for these cameras—this is your best bet. The B-Roll

About all those cheap cameras on Amazon: Readers often ask, why spend $400 on a GoPro when they can get a camera with 4K video for $100? It's a fair question, and the answer comes down to software, primarily image stabilization. Action cameras are designed to be strapped to helmets or clipped to your chest while you skydive, rock climb, or race through the city on a scooter. Without stabilization, the results are something even your closest friends won't sit through. So yes, you'll get 4K footage with the cheaper cams, but it'll be footage no one wants to see. In our view, you're better off spending another $100 for an older GoPro or DJI Osmo Action on sale.

Best Accessories to Trick Out Your Camera

Once you have an action camera, you're good to go for most use cases. GoPro, Insta360, and DJI all provide helmet mounts and other ways to stick your camera where you want it. But there are some nice extras that can make getting that shot you're dreaming of even easier. Here are a few:

A good microSD card: The action camera you buy may include an SD card. Insta360's SD cards are pretty good, actually, but I tend to use SanDisk's Extreme cards because they're fast and waterproof. You can pick up a 512-GB card for around $110. DJI has some specific recommendations for microSD cards to use with the Action 6. Of their list, the one I recommend is the Lexar Professional Silver Plus 512-GB card ($165). Sadly, like all flash-memory products, a shortage has led to skyrocketing microSD card prices.

GoPro Media Mod ($100): The Hero 13 Black's sound capture is OK out of the box, but if you want higher-quality audio, this is where you start. Not because the Media Mod mic captures great sound—it's better than the camera, but still not great. What it offers is a microphone jack. Plug in a high-quality microphone, and you'll finally have awesome sound. GoPro has listened to users and wisely included this feature out of the box for the Mission 1 Pro, which will have a media mod as well, but not necessary just to connect external audio.

Handlebar/seatpost/pole mount ($40): This is my favorite mount for mountain biking, but it'll also work on ski poles and any other round object you want to clamp it to.

Motorcycle accessory bundle ($60): If you ride, this mount for Insta360 cameras (Ace Pro, X5, etc.) is a must-have. It's one of the most secure clamps I've ever used, almost as good as a bench vise.

Yallsame tripod mount ($12): Action cameras offer many ways to mount them, but one that's curiously missing is the traditional quarter-inch tripod screw mount. The GoPros have them, but this three-pack of adapters solves the problem for the others.

Polarizer and ND filters: If you shoot around water, through glass, or in any other high-glare situation, a polarizing filter will help cut that glare. Neutral density (ND) filters hold back light to let you shoot at a wider aperture in bright light, helping to increase the amount of intentional motion blur. Both are great additions to your action camera kit. Here are the filters by camera:

  • GoPro Mission 1 ND filters ($100): These are hot off the press, and I have not tested them yet, but they work the same way the Hero 13 ND filters below do, automatically adjusting the camera settings when they are attached. This is a godsend for most of us since figuring out the best settings to use with an ND filter on is complicated. GoPro has done all the hard work for you.
  • GoPro Hero 13 Black ND filter Four-Pack ($90): These work like the Mission 1 ND filters above when it comes to automatic adjustment, but I have used these quite a bit. They're great for increasing the motion blur in fast shots on bright, sunny days—especially if there's snow on the ground.
  • DJI ND filter set for the Action 6 ($79): The Action 6 does not automatically detect and adjust settings, but depending on what you're shooting, there are plenty of guides online to getting good results with these filters.
  • Insta360 Ace Pro 2 ND Filters ($122): These strangely expensive filters work well with the Ace Pro 2.
  • Third-party ND filters: These are very hit-or-miss. Beware of slip-on filters as they often cause internal reflections. Others I've tried have had some pretty bad vignetting. For older GoPros, I like Freewell's polarizing and ND filters ($20). I suggest starting with an 8-stop ND or combo ND and polarizing filter.

Floaty ($35): Another one for the water shooters, but well worth the money since it'll keep your GoPro from ending up at the bottom of the sea. I haven't tried it, but there's one for the Insta360 Ace Pro 2 ($26) as well. DJI doesn't make an official case for the Action 6, but there is a floating grip ($59).

Selfie stick ($25): Surprisingly for someone who hates being on camera, I've tested a lot of selfie sticks, and they're almost all fine, but I keep grabbing this Insta360 version when I head out the door. It's lightweight, small enough to fit at the bottom of my bag, and it's affordable. If you want to go big, this Insta360 Extended Edition Selfie Stick ($100) can imitate a low-flying drone, perfect for use in national parks and other places where drones are forbidden.
