At the very end of 2025, my wife and I bought our first home, a 1907 Craftsman in the heart of Portland, Oregon. It’s a beautiful old house, listed on the National Historic Registry, and is largely unchanged from the day it was built. This is exactly what we dreamed of in a house. It’s loaded with history and vibes, mostly unmessed with across the decades. We’re now simultaneously updating and restoring the home with a limited budget, big ideas, our own style, and plenty of sweat equity.

The place we moved into was the level opposite of a “smart home”—and figuring out thoughtful ways to integrate modern technology into this old house without sacrificing aesthetics or ruining the vibe is the guiding principle.

We’re only getting started, but after a busy six months of learning, researching, and working cutting-edge technology into a house from the Roosevelt administration (Teddy, not FDR), I’ve kept a running log of the tech choices we’ve made along the way. Today, our house is starting to feel simultaneously old and new, which is very much the goal. It’s a lot of work, but I assure you it’s also a wonderful time.

Replacing the home’s heating system was a day one fix—our furnace was kicking off carbon monoxide and needed to be addressed immediately. But for daily use, we paired our spiffy new furnace with Ecobee’s new, midrange Ecobee Enhanced smart thermostat, which has a larger touchscreen than the basic Essential model and built-in radar for motion detection.

In making this choice, I consulted WIRED’s in-depth review of the Ecobee, which prioritizes the unit’s ease of setup and intuitive interface—this made a strong case for selecting the Ecobee over other similar smart thermostats, like the Google Nest, which lacks the Ecobee’s presence sensors.

The Ecobee gives us a monthly recap of our furnace usage and the average temperature in our home; walking into a low home-energy-score environment in a home that lacks insulation and gets drafty, having this monthly recap has been helpful already. Many states have a smart thermostat rebate program through local gas and power companies, and right now we’re earning around $10 off each monthly bill for having a smart thermostat in the house.

The Ecobee sits unobtrusively in our dining room; I’ve toyed with adding a vintage-leaning thermostat backing plate into the mix (Atomic Surplus makes really cool ones), though most of these are more midcentury than early 20th century. For now, a pop of digital in our old dining room doesn’t look too out of place.

Installing a proper whole-home air conditioning system into our home—which had never been done before—proved dauntingly cost-prohibitive as a day one update. At some point we will likely upgrade the air cooling system for the whole house, but these Midea units have made it possible to delay that choice without sacrificing comfort in our sleeping space.

The toilets in our home had been updated (at some point in the ’70s), but left much to be desired. Happily for all involved, shortly after moving in I undertook a comprehensive overhaul of WIRED’s authoritative guide to bidet technology. The glimmering jewel of all that good-natured buttmaxxing was an opportunity to install the Toto Aurora Washlet+ 7A with Integrativity System, which is without qualm or peer the nicest toilet I have ever experienced.

This mighty throne seamlessly integrates Toto’s universally well-regarded S7A bidet, providing automatic seat lifting and lowering and auto-flush. When paired together, these functions let you use your toilet without ever touching a button. The nozzle, heated seat, air dry, and oscillation capabilities are outstanding. You can also pair oscillation with a pulse action on the S7A for a noticeably more thorough experience.

I went ahead and hired a plumber for this installation, and he helped me navigate some of the quirks involved in putting a brand new high-tech unit into ancient pipes—in particular, the distance from the back wall to the toilet flange required a different size of “rough-in” than what comes standard from Toto. I also had to install a new electrical outlet near the toilet, which involved hiring an electrician.

But my goodness, was it all worth it. The innovative Integravity System cleans and refreshes the toilet each time you flush. Toto calls this “dual phase cleaning,” which reduces the time between flushing and rinsing to ensure the bowl sparkles after every use. It also only uses one gallon of water per flush, which is well below the Environmental Protection Agency standard of 1.6 gallons per flush.

Now the primary upstairs bathroom in which it sits is subtly perhaps the nicest and most complete room in the entire house. Does the Toto S7A “look vintage” in any way? No. Frankly, I think a person in 1907 would be positively astonished by the experience of using this technology, which is a funny thing to ponder. But our goal is to create a home that feels simultaneously old and new at the same time, and accepting compromises along the way. With this in mind, the S7A feels essential; something about its sleek, modern form creates a sort of pleasing aesthetic friction in our old house, particularly in the updated 1970s bathroom in which it resides.

Maybe the coolest thing about our house is the yard, a jewel-box quarter acre on the corner of our lot. It was more or less in a state of disrepair when we took over, blanketed in a thick layer of English ivy (vile weed), badly in need of arboreal trimming and care, and totally unmanaged in terms of water flow and irrigation.

I personally undertook the first huge tranche of work on this project: yanking out around 40 Home Depot garbage bags worth of the cursed ivy by the roots, reviving the dead soil by way of “the lasagna method” (Patricia Lanza’s book was a crucial housewarming gift from my mother-in-law), and then replanting most of the yard using a wild meadow mix from Northwest Meadowscapes. A few months later, I’m happy to report we have blooms, the bugs and birds have returned, and we’re able to start dreaming about what might come next.

Crucial to the meadow planting process has been the use of an OtO smart sprinkler, a movable automatic watering unit that sets up via app and can be placed strategically around the yard. This is ideal for a large yard lot, which presents a series of irrigation challenges; someday we may upgrade to an in-ground system, but for now we consulted WIRED’s guide to smart sprinklers and went with reviewer Christopher Null’s qualified recommendation around OtO: pint-sized, intelligent, and solar-powered, with only a garden hose required to get started.

“Most smart sprinklers are really smart controllers that need an in-ground system already in place,” says Brit Sastrawidjaya of Blueprint Earth, an ecological landscape designer based in Oregon, who confirmed the utility of the OtO for a new garden. “A smart hose timer just runs one setting wherever you set the sprinkler down. OtO lets you set different zones from one spot, and that matters, because every part of a garden waters differently while it's establishing.”

The app is fairly simple to use; programming the watering area had a slight learning curve and required some adjustment and experimentation to find what worked best, but as the yard has grown it’s been intuitive to further dial in the system and continue adjusting parameters. OtO allows you to set a watering schedule, turn the sprinkler on and off remotely, and set the depth of water you’d like to achieve.

We’ve supplemented OtO with hand watering, especially on hot days, but for the most part, OtO is handling our watering needs beautifully so far. Best of all, the OtO’s built-in solar panel is great—we’ve left it unplugged all summer after a single full charge to get things started.
