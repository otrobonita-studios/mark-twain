The era of
 plastic-free drip coffee has finally arrived. It couldn't come soon enough.
Drip coffee is my 
first true love
 when it comes to caffeine. But sadly, my first love is hopelessly addicted to plastic. From the very first Mr. Coffee machines in the 1970s, nearly every drip coffee maker on the market, including many of my 
favorite drip coffee makers
, has included plastic at some point in the brewing process.
But plastic, as we're now learning, can leach tiny particles 
called microplastics
 into coffee or water, which you then happily 
drink in your morning cup.
 The science isn't all in on health risks, but it doesn't 
seem
 great.
Luckily, in the past year, a couple new machines have arrived with the promise to remove plastic from the equation: the 
Ratio Eight Series 2 ($799)
 and the 
Simply Good Coffee Plastic-Free Coffee Brewer ($480)
. Each promises to deliver delicious, high-end coffee without the plastic.
Each costs significantly more than machines made with injection-molded plastic parts that are easier to manufacture. And each has a couple quirks and trade-offs. But the Ratio Eight in particular makes drip coffee that rivals the best pour-over, and the best drip coffee I've ever tasted—and never brings hot water into contact with plastic.
But for those who want to avoid plastic completely, including on the cold water tank, there's really only one drip coffee maker I've tested that fits the bill: the Simply Good Coffee plastic-free brewer, which comes in at a much lower price.
If flavor is a primary consideration, I'd still recommend upgrading to the Ratio Eight. It's also the coffee maker that's become my daily driver when testing new coffee roasters and my favorite 
coffee subscriptions
. But I understand that its price brings it out of reach for some. Here's the rundown.

Simply Good Coffee is a fairly new name in the coffee world, founded in 2022 by former Bonavita CEO Laura Sommers. Until last year, it was also a reasonably obscure brand, devoted mostly to making more affordable takes on premium, precision coffee makers like the Bonavita and Moccamaster, whose design Simply Good Coffee somewhat resembles.
The plastic-free machine rocketed Simply Good into sudden prominence—and indeed, it's sold out its production runs more than once since its release last year, riding a wave of interest in plastic-free drip coffee makers. The design is similar to Simply Good's previous model, but with plastic parts removed from the equation.
The frame is made of 304 “food grade” stainless steel. The reservoir, carafe, and boiler tube are borosilicate glass. Certain connections, including the drip stop, are made with medical-grade, sand-based silicone fittings that are not thought to produce nanoparticles easily. It is, quite simply, the least plasticky drip coffee machine I am aware of. There's no plastic contact, hot or cold, and very minimal silicone contact.

Operation is dead simple. There are, in fact, just two switches. One turns on the brewer and the warming plate; the other controls the pour-over style “bloom” function, adding a small phase to wet the grounds before brewing a full batch. The machine will turn itself off after 40 minutes, or you can do so manually.
So how's the coffee? It's good. It's generally light-bodied, brewing in six minutes even on full 40-ounce batches. It's tested, but not certified, to maintain precise brewing temperatures throughout the entire brewing process. The results are better than most basic drip coffee makers, but don't quite keep pace with the best-of-the-best coffee makers: the Moccamasters, the Fellows, the Ratios. There's a coffee warmer, which I don't prefer, but many do.
Some parts aren't machined as precisely as I'd hope, leading to a little jiggle or awkward fits. And customer service is also a lesser-known quantity with Simply Good. Calls to the customer service center during business hours reached only AI operators. But I did receive a call back a couple days later from someone I believe to be human. And the replacement warranty is an excellent five years.
In the end, if you're buying this, you're buying it because, for now, it is the drip coffee maker that is the 
most
 dedicated to ensuring that your coffee maker isn't adding microplastics to your cup. If 
you filter your water for microplastics
, the Simply Good Coffee will likely not add any microplastics after the fact. That's a lot of peace of mind.

The Simply Good Coffee brewer, like the Moccamaster it resembles, has been tested for compliance with “Golden Cup” parameters laid out by the Specialty Coffee Association, designed to describe best coffee brewing practices.
These include, but aren't limited to, the ability to keep coffee within tight time and temperature parameters while brewing—specifically, between 92 and 96 degrees Celsius. Each batch also remains within optimum times, brewing even a full batch in about six minutes. Simply Good received independent lab testing showing its capability to stay within tight brewing parameters. But it did not go through the far more rigorous and expensive SCA certification process.
This effort places Simply Good squarely in the “premium” category, and the coffee is going to taste better than most standard drip machines whose temperature can range wildly over the course of the brewing process. However, while the coffee tastes good, it doesn't taste as good as the Ratio—nor any of my other top drip coffee maker picks.
I noted some trouble with wetting coffee grounds evenly, especially on full 40-ounce batches, which can lead to under-extraction in some places and over-extraction in others. Which is to say: less precision of flavor, and a certain flatness. I didn't detect off notes, but I've also never had a cup with the Simply Good that I've been extremely excited about. And I've brewed dozens of batches.
Note that the warming plate gets quite warm, and it stays on during the brewing process, which means the coffee gets overheated during the early phases of brewing, leading to a small bit of diner-coffee oxidation in every batch—even if you take the carafe off after the batch completes.
Most coffee drinkers won't notice these quibbles much. But aficionados will. And among high-end coffee makers topping $400, Simply Good quite simply does not taste as good.
Flavor: 7/10
Ease of Use and Cleaning

Like the Moccamaster it resembles, the Simply Good machine has almost no buttons. Its operation is mostly as simple as turning it off and on, and choosing whether you'd like a “bloom” function that mimics pour-over.
But also like the Moccamaster, there are a lot of little parts: a reservoir lid, a shower attachment, a detachable brew chamber, a top for the chamber, a carafe with a detachable lid. Not all of these fit perfectly, or comfortably. The showerhead attachment has a little wobble to it.
The lid to the carafe can grind a bit, metal on glass, if you put it on even slightly crookedly—an uncomfortable form of friction that makes me worry about longevity. More parts also means more parts to clean. And while they can be put in the dishwasher, I doubt you'll do so daily.
The drip stop is both helpful and a potential hazard, depending on who you are. If you don't use the drip stop, there'll be a bit of dripping after the main brew. But if you do, there's a risk you won't notice it's still activated when you go to brew a batch. This'll cause an overflow if you let it. Entirely user error. And yet I've done it more than once.
Still, simple is simple. And it's dead simple to use, and hard to screw up. And stainless steel and borosilicate glass both clean quite easily.
Ease: 7/10

The 
Ratio Eight Series 2 ($799)
 is a beautiful machine. Its brew basket and carafe are as hourglass-shaped as a 1960s movie bombshell. The machine is sleek-lined, walnut-trimmed. The stainless steel brew basket and even the lid to the reservoir arrive with pleasing heft.
The coffee that arrives from it is also artful. Ever since Portland-based Ratio released the original Ratio Eight in 2013, the company has been on the forefront of artful, aromatic drip coffee—drip coffee that can compete with pour-over as a vehicle for beautiful aromatics and flavor. Ratio previously certified all of its machines according to Specialty Coffee Association standards, and all are capable of maintaining excellent temperature precision.
But in some ways, Ratio has moved beyond the SCA, after testing extractions on batch after batch. Ratio brews a little longer and extracts a bit more fully—as long as nine minutes for a full 40-ounce batch, with a leisurely bloom cycle. The showerhead leads to admirably even wetting of coffee grounds and even extraction. The results are full body, round flavor, and a surprising wallop even from quite delicate beans.

Controls are as simple as whispering your hand across the device’s single button. Press longer, and the machine will optimize itself for half batch, its button glowing faintly and satisfyingly.
This excellent coffee flavor is also evident in the Ratio's other devices, whether the 40-ounce Ratio Six or the small-batch Ratio Four. Each of these is available for a much lower price than the Eight.
Aesthetics aside, the Eight's true reason for being is that it minimizes plastic contact. Neither hot water, nor hot coffee, ever comes into contact with plastic. The internal tubing is borosilicate glass. The pipe connectors are sand-based silicone. The chassis is stainless steel, and so is the brewing basket.
This does not mean it's plastic-free. Its cold-water reservoir is a BPA-free Tritan copolymer. The machine's frame is also made of polymer, a decision made for durability over and above painted stainless steel. There’s a bit of BPA-free plastic used in the heating element that doesn’t come into contact with hot water.
Some will not forgive that the reservoir is plastic, even as they might acknowledge it's nice not to have to worry about fragility. Contact time is minimal—and where coffee is concerned, leaching occurs almost entirely when hot water is in contact.
I privilege the beauty of the coffee and of the device. But for some, the plastic chassis and reservoir amount to too much polymer compromise. And while the device's operation is simple, note that the brew basket does steam up the top of the device, meaning you might want to wipe it with a dry cloth. Because there's no drip stop, the brew basket also comes with a little drip tray to place it on.
But as with all beauty, I accept the idiosyncrasies that come with it. In these weary times of 
plastic in my liver and gonads
, this has become the drip coffee maker I use most often.
Plastic Content
AccordionItemContainerButton
The Ratio Eight does not bring hot liquid in contact with plastic, which is why I've included it as a plastic-free brewer. But plastic exists, notably in the BPA-free Tritan polymer of its cold-water reservoir.
A 
study published last year
 showed pretty clearly that hot-water contact with plastics is the main source of microplastic leaching in coffee. But long-term contact with cold water and light can still lead to some leaching, as evidenced by the microplastics content in bottled water. I'm not concerned about the 10 minutes or so at a time that cold water is in the reservoir, but some might be.
Much of the frame of the Ratio Eight Series 2 is also plastic, a decision made in part for durability: Steam and moisture are actually pretty hard on painted stainless steel. There’s a bit of BPA-free plastic used in the heating element that doesn’t come into contact with water. But though it's not relevant to coffee brewing, the Eight's plastic exterior doesn't fully pass the vibes check on a plastic-avoidant brewer.
Otherwise, the internal tubing is borosilicate glass, the brewing basket is hefty stainless steel, and the carafes are either stainless steel or borosilicate glass. While pipe fittings are silicone, The Eight arguably has less silicone contact than Simply Good's plastic-free brewer on the hot side of brewing, and less chance for mechanical wear at those sites.
Platic-Free Content: 8/10
Coffee Flavor
AccordionItemContainerButton
Flavor is where Ratio excels across all of its brewers. Despite single-button operation, and limited customization compared to a sophisticated drip brewer like the 
Fellow Aiden
, Ratio pulls downright shocking amounts of flavor from light and medium-roast coffee beans.
The key, in consultation with Ratio's designers and owners, is an obsessive-compulsive yen for extraction testing, and constant iteration and experimentation. The temperature curves are carefully modulated, and a bit individual: Brew times are longer by a couple minutes with the Ratio than with other devices. The bloom cycle is quite leisurely.
The result is fullness. It is coffee that speaks its flavors loudly, and clearly, with a roundness I don't find often from other drip brewers. I do find that I might grind my coffee slightly more coarsely with Ratio than with some other comparable brewers, to avoid over-extraction.
But among showerhead brewers, the evenness of extraction in the Ratio Eight 2 is exemplary, leading to some of the best and most balanced cups I've had this year.
Flavor: 10/10

The Ratio Eight's operation is, quite simply, as simple as any drip coffee machine I've ever used. Press its only button quickly, and you'll be optimized for a full batch. Hold down the button for three seconds and you'll be optimized for a half-batch.
That's it. That's the instructions. The machine handles the rest. One button leads to beauty.
Still, there are a couple extra fiddly parts. Unlike a lot of machines, the brew basket is removable and must be stacked atop the carafe. Because there's no drip stop, you'll have to rest this in a cradle that comes with the machine, lest it dribble coffee onto your counter.
You also have to manage steam and condensation a bit. Namely, steam comes out while brewing, and causes condensation on the top of the frame. What Are the Health Risks From Microplastics in Coffee?
This is not an easy question to answer, because scientific research is still in the early stages. The study of microplastics in the human body is new, and long-term studies still aren't in.
First things first: Coffee is likely not the biggest source of microplastics in your life. According to 
a study last year,
 hot coffee contains orders of magnitude fewer microplastics than water that's been bottled in cheap plastic. It also contains fewer than tea, because of nylon teabags. Depending where you live, you may have more to fear from your tapwater—and should first look into a good 
stainless steel water filter.
The general consensus, however, is that we should not welcome any additional tiny plastic particles in our digestive tracts and bloodstream, unless we need to.
Plastic is not very chemically reactive. But microplastics can transport other bad things into the body, such as bacteria. They can also just kinda … irritate your body. When they enter your bloodstream, they can reach vital organs and set off inflammatory responses and oxidative reactions.
Depending on the organ, t
his can do all sorts of stuff:
 imbalance hormones, mess with reproductive function, or even cause nervous system damage. Cancer, of course, is also a risk that's heightened by inflammation. Some reports show 
microplastics might weaken the immune system.
 Others that they may 
weaken the bones.
And so while coffee is not the worst risk where microplastics are concerned, caution is also not the worst approach.
