A car charger is not a complicated device. The best USB car charger is the one that charges your phone quickly, does not overheat, allows multiple devices to charge, and remains compatible with whichever USB cable you (or your passengers) happen to have available. It is usually also a trusted brand, with certification to international standards—not a janky gas station charger that'll blow a fuse with an internal short or offer insufficient protection against dust and debris.

I put car chargers to the test with ammeters and phone apps, and on multi-hour road trips, to make sure each one lived up to its promises. The best car charger I've found is the three-port Anker 167.5W Nano ($38), which combines 100-watt fast-charging capability for auxiliary devices, plus dual-port USB-Cs, USB-A capability, and safety features that include overheat protection.

If you shun dangling cords, I offer the Anker 75W Fast Charger ($30) with slightly lower top amperage but a retractable cable. And for families whose kids can't live without constant tablets in the back seat, there's the DeWalt 72W Front and Back Seat Charger ($45).

For other power needs, check out WIRED's guides to the Best Portable Jump Starters, Best Power Banks, Best MagSafe/Qi2 Power Banks, and Best Laptop Power Banks or Best Portable Power Stations.

How I Selected and Tested USB Car Chargers

For this round of car-charger testing, I looked first for known quantities and trusted charging technology brands like Anker, Belkin, and Ugreen. I also ordered and tested devices that other credible sources had ranked as their favorite USB chargers, making sure that each charger is broadly available and easy to find. Needless to say, I only assessed and recommend UL-certified chargers, and avoided dodgy-looking brands: Short circuits in cars aren't fun, for me or for you.

I then set about testing the claims for each car charger, using ammeters for each port on each charger, testing with an ammeter while pulling max loads. I additionally used the Ampere phone app to test the peak draw for a Motorola Razr and an iPhone 13, and also tested to see whether the charger heated up while pulling max draw—both using the phone app and an infrared thermometer.

I tested with each charger's included cord, where relevant, but also with a fast-charging cable on USB-C devices that advertise this capability. For my top contenders among 100-watt fast chargers, I also tested to see how long it took to charge a Macbook Air M4 and my top-pick jump starter, the 4000A Wolfbox.

As with every device tested by the WIRED Reviews team, I subjected my top-pick chargers to real-world road tests. I went on multi-hour road trips with devices plugged in. I charged portable jump starters or laptops while also charging phones. I tested durability in real-world conditions that may also involve grubby fingers. I assessed each device not just for brute speed, but for versatility in different use cases. For now, I favor chargers that offer both USB-C and USB-A capability, so as not to leave any devices out in the cold.

I also checked up on customer reviews for each of these chargers. If I saw any customer complaints, I tried my darndest to replicate them. I jammed chargers in and out of the ports, and wobbled the chargers around in their sockets. I acted the way people act when they're fumbling for a device charger they're not looking at while driving.

Are Cheap USB Chargers Unsafe?

I've put a bit of emphasis on trusted brands while testing USB chargers, and this isn't by accident. The world is full of dodgy gas station or Amazon chargers, and bad cables, with insufficient electrical insulation and inconsistent current delivery.

Bad USB-C cables and electrical insulation are known fire hazards in the worst cases, but inconsistent voltage is also just bad for your phone or laptop battery over time. Poorly made chargers, connectors, and cables can have higher resistance (leading to overheating), bad or inconsistent connections, and poor protections against shorts and surges. All of these things are bad. At worst, they can fry your device or lower your battery longevity.

As a rule of thumb, look for language that a product is “UL-Listed” or "UL-Certified" not merely "UL-Classified" or “Recognized.” The latter two mean that a product has been tested only against specific hazards or under specific conditions. Listed and Certified products have fully passed all tests within a given standard. WIRED will only recommend chargers that are UL-listed or -certified.
