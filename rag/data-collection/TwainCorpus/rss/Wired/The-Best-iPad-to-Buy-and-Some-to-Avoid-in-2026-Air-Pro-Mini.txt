FINDING THE BEST
 iPad should be simple. You get whatever’s new, right? If only. Apple sells four main iPad models in varying sizes, each with its own strengths. In addition, a growing number of older iPads are floating around the eBays of the world. And to make things more complex, Apple recently announced a price increase across its entire lineup, further complicating knowing which model to buy in 2026.
This guide covers the iPad models currently available, what’s coming up, the key differences between them, and the older versions that are still in use (including those you should avoid at any price). I also break down which Apple Pencil and accessories to pair with your device.

AccordionItemContainerButton
There are tons of iPads on the market. Knowing which model you own is important if you’re considering selling the iPad or if you're 
looking for accessories
 that are compatible with your specific device. Further complicating the matter of identifying your iPad model is that—unlike with the iPhone—Apple doesn't name its iPads sequentially
Nonetheless, finding your iPad model is simple. Here’s how to do it, in two steps:
Look at the back of your iPad. Etched on the back cover, near where it says "Designed by Apple in California," you should see a model number starting with the letter A.
Head to 
this website from Apple
. Look for your corresponding model number, and that's it! You found your iPad model. (Pro tip: Use your browser's "Find in Page" function to type in your model number and find it quickly.)
For accessories, retailers usually indicate which iPad model(s) a given case, folio, or keyboard cover is designed to work with. Consider the iPad’s Compatible Operating System

iPadOS 27
Apple announced the forthcoming 
iPadOS 27

Much of the focus of the operating system seems to be on Siri. iPadOS 27 will introduce a dedicated Siri AI app that uses the world knowledge and context of Google Gemini. The update also improves integration with apps including Camera, Mail, Photos, Messages, Calendar, and more. The most interesting of its AI features is Shortcuts, which will let you create an automated task using natural language that can trigger actions across multiple apps.
iPadOS 27 will also refine the highly controversial Liquid Glass redesign from last year's iPadOS 26, adding a slider in Settings that allows you to change the opacity. Other notable changes in this year's upcoming software update include 
faster, more efficient performance

Avoid These iPads
AccordionItemContainerButton
The iPad Mini 1 through 6 and iPad 1 through 10 either have low-resolution, pixelated screens or are just too old. Forget buying a 2014 iPad Air 1 or a 2015 Air 2, as those are simply too long in the tooth. In fact, I would avoid purchasing a device that came out before 2020.
There’s a chance an older iPad could perform decently, but unless someone is giving you one for free, you’re better off hunting for a newer model at a reduced price. Many of the older iPads no longer receive software updates, and they'll seem quite slow compared to your phone and your computer.

Should You Buy an Older iPad Pro?
AccordionItemContainerButton
Apple has always packed a little extra into its iPad Pro tablets compared to its base, Air, and Mini models. It's still totally fine to buy the 
iPad Pro M4
, 
iPad Pro M2
, or the 
iPad Pro M1
 if you can find them, but just make sure you're not paying close to MSRP. If it's close to what you would pay for a brand-new M5 iPad Pro, the older model isn’t worth the small savings. If you can snag a good deal on one of the aforementioned slightly dated models, you’ll get a powerful machine that can match the current Pro slates in many ways; these devices also work with the first-generation 
Magic Keyboard with Trackpad
.
Older iPad Pro models, 
like the models from 2018
 or 2020, are not worth buying (if they're even available). They're just too old.

Should You Get an iPad or MacBook?
AccordionItemContainerButton
Essentially, the iPad is primarily for entertainment and content consumption, while the MacBook is for work, school, and content creation. You can use both devices for any of those previously stated purposes, especially as Apple expands its product lines. But each shines brightest in its intended uses. That's why iPads work so well for kids, retired folks, and for bringing on vacation.
The iPad Pro, however, may be an outlier, as it is truly a pro-level content-creation device. There is a demographic out there that has a use for its tandem OLED display, faster ports, and M5-level graphics, but it's a much smaller niche.

The classic iPad was never meant to be a product for serious work—at least not in the conventional sense. But over the past decade, iPad software and accessory upgrades have made the devices more capable of tasks formerly reserved for a laptop or desktop. This evolution started with cursor and mouse support, then came the Magic Keyboard with a full trackpad and an extra port. Then, 
iPadOS 26
 brought windowed multitasking. For students or anyone else who wants to use those aforementioned features to do more with their iPad, I recommend the 
M4 iPad Air
 (7/10, WIRED Recommends).
Contrary to what its name implies, the iPad Air is not actually much thinner or lighter than the base iPad. Instead, the iPad Air lets you use an iPad as your primary work or school device. Not only does it offer a significantly better performance than the base iPad for tasks like video editing or gaming, but it also lets you bump up to the 13-inch screen size for a larger canvas to work with. Whether you want to use your iPad to do art in Procreate or just write many emails, you'll appreciate that extra screen space. The 12 GB of RAM means you can feel free to have many apps open, and you can even dabble in Apple Intelligence or other on-device AI tasks.
Another reason to get the iPad Air is its compatible accessories. The large 
Magic Keyboard
 that comes with the 13-inch iPad Air offers a spacious typing experience. The key spacing and keycap size are close to those of a MacBook’s keyboard. The trackpad and palm rests are still a little small, but the keyboard itself is great.

Which Apple Pencil Should You Buy?
Photograph: Julian Chokkattu
The Apple Pencil is a must-have for any iPad owner who takes a lot of notes or likes to draw. (You can even use it to sign documents.) iPadOS also has handwriting support within certain search fields and apps (like Notes), so you don't need to switch to the keyboard every time you want to type. Apple debuted a new Pencil in 2024, so now there are four options from which you can choose. Here are the key differences:
Apple Pencil (first gen) for $99
:
 The first-generation Pencil is quite old but still functions well, offering pressure sensitivity (lines become thicker as you press down harder on the screen) and tilt sensitivity (for shading). Unlike the other two Apple Pencils, this one has a glossy finish instead of matte (if that matters), and it is also completely rounded, meaning it will roll off surfaces. It doesn’t feature magnets, so you can't stick it to the tablet for safekeeping. It has a Lightning connector, so to charge it, you'll have to remove the cap and plug it into the iPad's charging port. If your iPad has a USB-C port, you can buy 
an adapter
. It's only compatible with the iPad (sixth gen and later), iPad Air (third gen), iPad Mini (fifth gen), iPad Pro 12.9-inch (first and second gen), iPad Pro 10.5-inch, and iPad Pro 9.7-inch.
Apple Pencil (USB-C) for $71
:
 The USB-C Apple Pencil is a bit perplexing. It doesn't come with pressure sensitivity, so it's better suited for note-takers than artists. It can attach magnetically to the sides of an iPad, so you don't need to find a place to store it. However, it doesn't support wireless charging, so you'll have to pair and charge it via USB-C. It supports Hover Mode, which allows you to preview what will happen before touching the display. This feature is exclusive to the select iPad Pro and iPad Air models. The USB-C Apple Pencil is compatible with iPad (10th gen and A16), iPad Air (fourth gen, fifth gen, M2, M3, and M4), iPad Mini (sixth gen), iPad Pro 11-inch (first gen and later, including M4), and iPad Pro 12.9-inch (third gen and later, including M4 and M5).
Apple Pencil (second-gen) for $129
:
 The second-generation Apple Pencil has the same capabilities as the first-gen and USB-C versions, plus wireless pairing and charging (it'll charge when it magnetically sticks to the top edge of supported iPads). It also adds a double-tap feature that lets you quickly switch between tools by tapping on the Apple Pencil twice. It's 
compatible with
 the iPad Air (fourth and fifth gen), iPad Mini (sixth gen), iPad Pro 11-inch (first gen and later), and the 12.9-inch (third gen and later). Unfortunately, Apple officially discontinued it, so newer iPads—including the iPad Air (M2, M3, and M4) or iPad Pro (M4 and M5)—don't support it.
Apple Pencil Pro for $99
:
 Apple's Pro Pencil is the most feature-packed of them all. It looks identical to the second-generation model, complete with a matte finish, but it also has a few new tricks. A “squeeze” feature opens up a tool palette, and a new haptic engine gives feedback as you squeeze. A built-in gyroscope sensor allows you to change the orientation of the tools as you twist them (for finer control), and it supports Apple's Find My network to track the stylus if you lose it. It's only compatible with the iPad Air (M2, M3, and M4), iPad Pro (M4 and M5), and seventh-gen iPad Mini. (
