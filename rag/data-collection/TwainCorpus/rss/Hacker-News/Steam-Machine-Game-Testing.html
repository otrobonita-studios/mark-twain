We finally have the Steam Machine in our hands, and it feels good to hold another mainstream gaming device that ships with Linux. The Steam Deck put Linux in the hands of countless gamers, and abandoning Windows for Linux was finally a real option for many who previously thought it a joke. We recently test-drove a few gaming-oriented distros, and the Linux gaming experience is good these days. Valve is doubling down on its SteamOS bet with the Steam Machine, and the gamble seems to be paying off.

    Front and back of the Steam Machine

    Physically, there is a lot to like. It is a tiny, cute form factor affectionately called the GabeCube in many circles. The magnetically attached front plate can easily be removed or swapped out; Valve showcased a few options, including an e-ink display on the front. Valve released CAD files for the Steam Deck, which enables endless possibilities, and we bet they will release the same for the Steam Machine.

    We put the Steam Machine through some testing before we handed it back to David and Linus to review in the LTT video linked below.

    Hardware Behind the Software

    The trouble with the Steam Machine is that software is only as good as the hardware behind it, and the Steam Machine’s platform disappoints for a mid-2026 release.

    Source: https://store.steampowered.com/hardware/steammachine

    The Steam Machine’s processor and graphics are decidedly last generation. The semi-custom CPU is a Zen 4, six-core, twelve-thread design with SMT, which seems perfectly fine. It has a 30 W TDP and is reported to boost up to 4.8 GHz. It would have been nice for the Steam Machine to be powered by newer Zen 5c cores, as found in Strix Point mobile processors, but the semi-custom chip is plenty of juice for a processor targeting 1080p to 4K resolutions (where the processor matters the least). It's close desktop equivalent is the Ryzen 5 7500F, albeit based on specifications rather than any comparative testing yet – future plans!

    The Ryzen 5 7500F can boost up to 5.0 GHz and, at 65 W, pulls twice as much power as the Steam Machine’s processor. In our testing a year ago, we observed the 7500F delivering nearly the same FPS as a 9800X3D at 4K, and it was competent at 1080p. Even at half the power, the semi-custom processor of the Steam Machine should be plenty for a gaming-first machine.

    The most disappointing and impactful specification is the Steam Machine’s last-gen graphics package. Surprisingly, its 8 GB of VRAM in 2026 isn’t the most controversial part of its graphics story. Instead, the scandal is the already aged RDNA3-based graphics processor. 

    The GabeCube comes with 28 compute units, is based on RDNA3, and has a sustained clock of 2.45 GHz at a 110 W TDP.  The closest discrete card comparison would be the RX 7600 8GB, which has 4 more compute units (32 total) and can draw up to 165 W of power.

    When we tested the RX 7600 back in 2023, yes, it's that old, we were adequately whelmed by its performance. The RX 7600 was decidedly not a 4K gaming card, even with the FSR of its time. The Steam Machine isn’t set up for success, given that Valve is advertising these specs as capable of 4K 60 FPS, with the caveat that FSR is required (more on that later). FSR has come a long way since 2023, so there may be some hope for the Steam Machine’s performance, but we went into testing less hopeful than we would have liked.

    Gaming Results

    For those that read about our gaming distro exploration, you will know that it’s important to note the Mesa and Proton version present during the game testing. In this case Steam defaulted to the Proton Experimental branch, and Mesa was 26.1.2.

    1080p

    We started with our standard suite, which calls for maximum settings, and it turned out to be pretty rough for the Steam Machine’s limited hardware. In lieu of Presentmon, we captured frame data using Mangohud with a logging interval of 0.

    You can see the exact settings we used in the game settings appendix.

    1080p Highest Preset Results

    Cyberpunk 2077 and Shadow of the Tomb Raider hit a respectable 60 FPS at 1080p, but you won’t be cranking the settings to maximum for all games on the Steam Machine’s meagre hardware. Note that this is the last time you’ll see Shadow of the Tomb Raider running on Proton in our graphs; we stuck with the native build for the rest of this project.

    We continued lowering the settings at 1080p to see how low you have to go to get 60 FPS on the other titles.

    1 of 3

      Game results from across different graphic presets

      There are a few reports of Anno 177: Pax Romana struggling on modest hardware, but we hit 60 FPS on the Medium preset and could go lower. DOOM: The Dark Ages required us to go to the bottom of the preset barrel, setting it to Low, while Forza Horizon 6 hit 60 FPS at a respectable High preset.

      This is by no means an exhaustive list of games and presets, and within a graphics menu, some settings will affect your FPS more than others. From our experience, shadow, light, and texture-related settings tend to be prime suspects.

      The Steam Machine can deliver an enjoyable 1080p experience, but you’ll have to trade some graphics quality for performance or enable FSR. That’s what we did to challenge the playable 4K experience Valve is claiming.

      2160p Upscaling

      In an effort to hit 60 FPS at 4K, we enabled FSR with Quality, Performance, and Ultra Performance settings. We used a mix of presets for the FSR Quality and FSR Performance tests, and the results aren’t promising. Shadow of the Tomb Raider is the only one that hit 60 FPS, and it doesn’t even have FSR; granted, this game is ancient by comparison to the others.

        To evaluate FPS, we enabled variable refresh rate (VRR) on all devices and then enabled FPS reporting on the ASUS ROG Swift OLED PG27AQWP-W, which reports the refresh rate. This isn’t totally accurate, but it gives a good enough representation of performance.

        PS5 Pro and PS5 versus Steam Machine

        The PS5 bounced between 69 and 86 FPS but hovered around 75-78 FPS. The PS5 Pro was between 100 and 120 FPS, but mostly rested around 110 to 112 FPS.

        Steam Machine Configuration 2, with the native 1440p settings, seems to perform slightly better despite the higher settings. FPS varied from 81 to 61, but it remained stable at around 70-73 FPS. We never saw it dip below 60 in this setup, so native 1440p TAA was better than upscaling from 1440p to 4K in this quick experiment.

        While we had the consoles set up, we also stuck a microphone next to them.

        The Form Factor

        Remove the Steam Machine’s casing, and you will be greeted with the massive heatsink, seriously this entire box is nearly 90% heatsink. The core is trivial to remove from the shell.  There are four bolts that need to be removed from the rear(two are captive, two on the fan housing), and there are four more bolts on the bottom of the shell, doubling as the rubber feet.

        The bottom of the Steam Machine

        As you can see from these profile shots of the naked cube the cooling situation is a single 120 mm fan pulling air through the top mounted heatsink which has two copper heat pipes snaking through it.

            Thermals and Power

            We used Mangohud to record the thermal and power states under both light and heavy game loads. We ran the Abyssal Forest benchmark in DOOM: The Dark Ages at 2160p and Low Preset for our heavy scenario. Tape to Tape at 2160p and max settings was our light game. We locked the FPS to 60 in both titles to make the results more comparable.

            Mangohud was not reporting cpu_power, so we only have gpu_power to draw in a line chart. 

            Power draw during DOOM: The Dark Ages and Tape to Tape

            However, we did use our Quarch QTL2843 to measure power from the wall. From the wall, during DOOM: The Dark Ages, the Steam Machine averaged about 176 W with a high of 185 W and a low of 157 W.

            Quarch screenshot during DOOM: The Dark Ages

            In DOOM, the semi-custom CPU and GPU each hit their advertised frequencies, 4.8 GHz and 2.4 GHz respectively. The single 120 mm fan is doing its job, the temperature during Abyssal Forest maxed out at 70°C.

                Noise Concerns

                We reused the opening scene from God of War Ragnarök for this test. Before we go further, this test is just as rough as the console FPS comparisons, so take these results lightly. We stuck our CM-140 SPL meter 5 cm away from the exhaust at a 45-degree angle. The room's noise floor was measured at 32.2 dB.

                Microphone setup with a PlayStation

                At idle, you can barely tell that the PS5 Pro or Steam Machine are on, as the noise is about equal to the noise floor. The PS5 measured at 33.3 dB and was audible.

                All Out Of Steam

                There is more testing and experimentation planned with the Steam Machine, but for now that’s what we've got. The Steam Machine is an excellent step forward in Valve’s mission to spread Linux adoption, even despite its performance flaws.

                Oh yeah, the price, it starts at $1,049 USD for the 512 GB and $1,349 USD for the 2 TB – then add $69 USD to either if you want a controller bundled in.
