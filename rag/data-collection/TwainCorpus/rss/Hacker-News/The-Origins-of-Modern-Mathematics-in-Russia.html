The Origins of Modern Mathematics in Russia: From Peter the Great to the Bernoullis and Euler

Valeriy Manokhin, PhD, MBA, CQF
13 min read
·
Dec 10, 2025

--

When we speak about the rise of Russian mathematics, we often imagine the brilliant flowering of the 19th and 20th centuries — the eras of Chebyshev, Lyapunov, Markov, Kolmogorov, and many others. But the roots of this tradition reach back much earlier, into an era when Russia had no systematic mathematical culture at all. The modern tradition began almost abruptly, through the ambitions of one ruler and the arrival of several foreign geniuses.

This story — part institutional history, part intellectual migration — shows how Russia became one of the world’s mathematical centers.

Peter the Great and the Birth of the Academy

Peter the Great had a deep, practical appreciation for mathematics — not as an abstract science, but as a tool for building fleets, armies, and a modern state. By the last year of his life, founding a Russian Academy of Sciences had become one of his most urgent projects.

He had long discussed the idea with leading European thinkers — Leibniz among them — and in 1724 he signed the decree establishing the Academy. Peter died soon after, and the Academy opened only posthumously, but his intent had already shaped its direction: mathematics must stand at its core.

At that time, the leading voice in selecting scholars for the new institution was Christian Wolff, a German philosopher and devoted disciple of Leibniz. And Wolff’s instincts would change the trajectory of Russian science.

Why Basel Became the Center of New Mathematics

After the breakthroughs of Newton and Leibniz — who formalized the calculus started by Fermat, Roberval, Huygens, Torricelli — something remarkable occurred. For a few decades, the forefront of mathematical analysis moved to an unlikely place: Basel, a small Swiss city.

There, in the early 18th century, lived the extraordinary Bernoulli family — Johann, Jakob, Daniel, Nikolaus — a dynasty of creative mathematicians whose work shaped the new calculus. And among their students and collaborators was a young, prodigious Leonhard Euler.

Wolff managed to do something visionary: he convinced two Bernoullis — Daniel and Nikolaus — to join the fledgling St. Petersburg Academy. And they, in turn, brought a promising twenty-year-old Euler to Russia.

Euler in Russia: The Giant Who Started a School

Euler arrived in St. Petersburg in 1727. In Russia he would spend 31 years in total, in two long periods separated by his stay in Berlin under Frederick II.

During his Russian years Euler:

  • mastered the Russian language
  • produced a substantial portion of his nearly 900 scientific works
  • laid the foundation for Russian mathematical education
  • trained the earliest generation of local mathematicians

His intellectual energy was legendary. Today, Euler’s Opera Omnia is planned as 72 massive volumes, about 62 of which have already been published — a global monument of scientific output. The edition is directed from Switzerland, with Russia providing original manuscripts.

Euler was not simply a visitor; he established the first true mathematical school in Russia.

Lomonosov and Euler: A Misunderstood Genius and His Defender

Mikhail Lomonosov worked in the Academy during the same years as Euler. Lomonosov was a brilliant, fiery, often abrasive figure — too early for Russia, in some sense. His country admired him as a poet but misunderstood him as a scientist.

At one moment, Academy officials, who disliked his temper, asked Euler to give a negative assessment of Lomonosov’s scientific writings. Instead, Euler declared them genius. This unexpected verdict thwarted attempts to discipline or marginalize Lomonosov — an episode that shows Euler’s intellectual integrity and the uneasy politics of the Academy.

Nineteenth-Century Russia: Ostregradsky, Bunyakovsky, Lobachevsky

After Euler and Lomonosov came the Russian-born generation.

Mikhail Ostrogradsky: The First Great Russian Mathematician

The first native mathematician whose work equaled that of leading Europeans. Active in the early 1800s, he became a foundational figure in analysis and mechanics.

What he discovered, what he invented, and why he mattered

When people say that Ostrogradsky was the first native Russian mathematician comparable to the leading European analysts, they mean something very specific: he created original, deep mathematical tools and theories — not merely learned or applied them — and he did so at the cutting edge of 19th-century science.

Below is a breakdown of his actual contributions.

⭐ 1. The Divergence Theorem (Gauss–Ostrogradsky Theorem)

If Ostrogradsky had done nothing else, this alone would secure his place in history.

  • Ostrogradsky independently gave the first full, rigorous formulation and proof, published in 1831–1834.
  • In Russian and French literature, the theorem is still called “Ostrogradsky’s formula.”

Impact

This theorem underlies:

  • electromagnetism (Maxwell’s equations)
  • fluid mechanics
  • heat flow
  • continuum mechanics
  • modern engineering and physics

It is one of the central identities of all applied mathematics.

⭐ 2. Foundations of Variational Calculus

Ostrogradsky was a direct intellectual descendant of Euler and Lagrange, and he pushed their ideas further.

He extended and systematized:

  • the Euler–Lagrange equation to higher-order derivatives
  • the theory of multiple integrals in variational problems
  • conditions for extrema in systems with constraints

His work is now used in:

  • classical mechanics
  • elasticity theory
  • optimal control
  • field theories in physics

Why this mattered

Before Ostrogradsky, variational calculus was a collection of brilliant isolated ideas. He turned it into a general methodology.

⭐ 3. Ostrogradsky’s Instability Theorem

This result, revived in the 20th and 21st centuries, is deep physics.

What it says

Any physical theory with a Lagrangian containing higher-than-first derivatives of time leads inevitably to instabilities(ghost states, unbounded energy).

Why it matters

This theorem constrains permissible physical theories. It is now cited in:

  • quantum field theory
  • modified gravity theories
  • cosmology (dark energy models)

Even today, modern physicists attempting to build new theories must check: Does your model violate Ostrogradsky’s condition? If so — it collapses.

⭐ 4. Advances in Heat Transfer, Hydrodynamics, and Elasticity

Ostrogradsky was one of the first true mathematical physicists in Russia. He wrote major works on:

  • heat conduction
  • fluid flow
  • elastic deformation
  • ballistics

He brought rigorous mathematics into engineering problems in the spirit of Euler.

⭐ 5. The First Russian School of Mathematical Analysis

Ostrogradsky was not only a researcher — he was a teacher who shaped the next generation.

His legacy includes:

  • raising the level of Russian mathematical education to European standards
  • mentoring Viktor Bunyakovsky
  • influencing early figures who later shaped Chebyshev’s circle

Before him, Russia had isolated talents (Lomonosov, some foreign academicians). After him, Russia had its own genuine analytical tradition.

Why Ostrogradsky Is a Turning Point

Before Ostrogradsky:Russia imported mathematics from Europe.

After Ostrogradsky:Russia produced mathematics that Europe had to pay attention to.

His work sits at the root of:

  • Russian mathematical physics
  • the St. Petersburg analytic tradition
  • the future development of Chebyshev, Markov, Lyapunov, Steklov, and others

He is the bridge from the Euler/Bernoulli era to the native Russian school.

Viktor Bunyakovsky: The Quiet Architect of Russian Number Theory and Probability

Viktor Yakovlevich Bunyakovsky (1804–1889) is one of those figures whose name appears often in footnotes yet whose true influence is far deeper than casual readers realize. He stood at a pivotal moment in Russian science, linking the era of Ostrogradsky to the generation of Chebyshev. He was not an explosive genius like Euler nor a revolutionary like Lobachevsky — but he was a powerful system-builder, a theorist of exceptional precision, and a stabilizing institutional force in the Russian Academy of Sciences.

Here is what he actually contributed.

⭐ 1. The Cauchy–Bunyakovsky Inequality (telegraphed throughout modern mathematics)

This inequality appears everywhere:

  • analysis
  • geometry
  • quantum mechanics
  • machine learning
  • Hilbert spaces
  • numerical methods
  • statistics

In school and Western literature it is usually called Cauchy–Schwarz or Cauchy–Bunyakovsky–Schwarz, but historically Bunyakovsky gave one of the earliest general proofs in the context of quadratic forms and vector-like structures.

It is one of the cornerstones of modern mathematical analysis.

⭐ 2. Founding the Russian tradition of rigorous number theory

Bunyakovsky’s 1857 monograph “Основания теории вероятностей” (“Foundations of Probability Theory”) is well known, but his work in number theory is, if anything, more prescient.

Most famous idea:

The Bunyakovsky Conjecture (1832)

A polynomial f(n)f(n) with integer coefficients, positive values, and irreducible over the integers should take prime values for infinitely many integers nn.

This is a powerful generalization of classical prime distribution questions.

For example:

  • n2+1
  • n3+2
  • 2n+1

should all hit primes infinitely often — if the conjecture is true.

It remains completely open today and is considered one of the most important unproven statements in analytic number theory, closely related to the Bateman–Horn Conjecture (its modern extension). He was over a century ahead of his time.

He also contributed to:

  • the geometry of numbers
  • theory of Diophantine equations
  • early analytic methods in arithmetic
  • distribution of prime values of functions

His work influenced Chebyshev’s early development.

⭐ 3. One of the earliest systematic texts on probability theory

Bunyakovsky published in 1846 what is arguably the first rigorous Russian textbook on probability, and one of the first such textbooks anywhere that tried to unify:

  • classical combinatorial probabilit
  • geometric probability
  • limit tendencies
  • expectations and mean values
  • statistical reasoning in physics and social sciences

He pressed for the idea that probability should become the mathematical foundation of statistical sciences — decades before this became orthodox.

He formulated early versions of:

  • the law of large numbers
  • methods for error estimation
  • probability-based decision making

This work anticipated aspects of later developments by Chebyshev, Markov, and Lyapunov. Without Bunyakovsky, the Russian probabilistic tradition (so dominant in the 20th century) might not have existed.

⭐ 4. Contributions to mathematical physics

Like Ostrogradsky, Bunyakovsky worked deeply in applied mathematics. His contributions include:

  • theory of elasticity
  • heat conduction
  • potential theory
  • mechanics of continua

His analytical techniques helped bridge the gap between pure and applied work at a time when Russian science was still forming its identity.

⭐ 5. A powerful institutional leader in the Academy

Bunyakovsky was not only a mathematician — he was a builder of institutions. For decades he served as:

  • an academician of the St. Petersburg Academy
  • a key figure in hiring, funding, and supporting research
  • a protector of mathematical publications and young scholars

He acted as a conservative but stabilizing presence during turbulent institutional politics, often opposing more radical or experimental trends but ensuring continuity and rigorous standards.

In the famous conflict with Lobachevsky, he took the “orthodox” side — which history now sees as a scientific mistake — but politically he represented the Academy’s dominant viewpoint of the time.

⭐ 6. A bridge between generations

Ostrogradsky and Bunyakovsky together formed the first stable Russian analytic school. Bunyakovsky’s students and collaborators helped shape the environment that later produced:

  • Chebyshev
  • Markov
  • Lyapunov
  • Voronoy

Although he is less famous than these giants, his influence runs through them.

Why Bunyakovsky Matters Today

Because:

  • The Cauchy–Bunyakovsky inequality is one of the pillars of modern analysis.
  • His probability theory work laid foundations for the later Russian school that dominated the 20th century.
  • His prime-polynomial conjecture is still one of the deepest unsolved problems in mathematics.
  • His institutional role helped stabilize the Academy during its formative decades.

He was not a flamboyant innovator, but he was among the core founders of Russian mathematical culture — rigorous, analytic, structured — the soil from which later breakthroughs grew.

Nikolai Lobachevsky: The Man Who Bent Geometry and Changed the World

Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky (1792–1856) is one of the rare figures in mathematics whose achievement is not merely a theorem or a method, but an entirely new way of thinking. Working in the relative isolation of Kazan University, far from the scientific capitals of Europe, he created non-Euclidean geometry — an intellectual earthquake whose aftershocks extend into modern physics, cosmology, and the foundations of mathematical logic.

Yet in his own time, especially in Russia, he was met with skepticism, mockery, or indifference. Only decades later did the world realize that Lobachevsky had altered the trajectory of mathematics.

⭐ 1. What Lobachevsky Actually Invented

Hyperbolic Geometry — the first complete non-Euclidean geometry in history

For more than 2,000 years, mathematicians wrestled with Euclid’s Fifth Postulate — the parallel postulate:

Through a point not on a line, exactly one line can be drawn parallel to the given line.

Everyone assumed it was true but awkward, hoping someday to derive it from simpler axioms.

Lobachevsky asked a revolutionary question:

What if the parallel postulate is not true?

What if:

  • through a point not on a line, more than one parallel line can be drawn?

Instead of treating this as absurd, he built an entire consistent geometry on this alternative assumption. The results were astonishing:

  • triangles have angle sums less than 180°
  • distances grow exponentially rather than linearly
  • lines behave like arcs of circles orthogonal to a boundary at infinity
  • geometry becomes deeply linked to complex analysis and group theory

This new geometry is now called hyperbolic geometry, or historically Lobachevskian geometry.

Why this matters

Lobachevsky proved something profound:

Geometry is not uniquely determined by physical space.You can construct multiple, perfectly consistent geometries.

This insight eventually reshaped:

  • Einstein’s general relativity
  • the philosophy of space and logic
  • the topology and geometry of surfaces
  • modern mathematical physics

Lobachevsky is one of the founders of the concept we now call mathematical pluralism.

⭐ 2. How he published — and how he was ignored

Lobachevsky first announced his ideas in 1826 and published them in:

  • Kazan University Courier (1829–1830)
  • Geometrical Investigations on the Theory of Parallels (1840, in German)

His early Russian publications had tiny circulation; the mathematics establishment barely noticed. His German monograph reached a slightly larger audience, but the reaction was mixed:

  • The few who read it found it unbelievable or unnecessary.
  • Most dismissed it as a mistake.

Meanwhile, in Hungary, János Bolyai developed essentially the same geometry independently. But neither Bolyai nor Lobachevsky gained widespread acceptance for decades.

⭐ 3. Why Russian mathematicians rejected him

The Russian mathematical community was small and deeply conservative. For them:

  • Euclid was sacred.
  • Geometry was “the science of the real world,” not a playground for philosophical experiments.
  • Deviating from the parallel postulate seemed nonsensical.

Thus:

  • Bunyakovsky considered Lobachevsky’s work misguided.
  • Ostrogradsky, though a brilliant analyst, dismissed non-Euclidean ideas as irrelevant.
  • Chernyshevsky, the literary critic, openly mocked Lobachevsky in print.

This was not malice — it was simply that Lobachevsky was too far ahead of his time.

He spent the last part of his life blind, exhausted, and largely unrecognized, eventually losing his position as rector of Kazan University.

⭐ 4. When the world finally understood

Lobachevsky’s redemption came only in the second half of the 19th century.

The turning point

In the 1860s–1870s, mathematicians like:

  • Beltrami
  • Klein
  • Poincaré

showed that Lobachevsky’s geometry is not only consistent but deeply embedded in the structure of analytic and topological mathematics.

Beltrami constructed explicit models of hyperbolic geometry inside Euclidean geometry — an idea neither Lobachevsky nor Bolyai had. This proved that their system was logically coherent.Klein and Poincaré then used it to develop transformation groups, automorphic functions, and the geometry underlying modern complex analysis.

Suddenly the mathematical world understood:

Lobachevsky had proposed one of the greatest conceptual revolutions since Euclid himself.

By the early 20th century, he was honored globally as the founder of non-Euclidean geometry. By mid-20th century, his name was inseparable from modern physics: the geometry of Einstein’s universe has non-Euclidean curvature.

⭐ 5. Why Lobachevsky matters today

Because:

  • Modern cosmology uses curved geometries derived from his ideas.
  • Topology, group theory, and complex analysis rely on hyperbolic geometries.
  • Computer science, especially in network theory and data visualization, uses hyperbolic models.
  • Cryptography explores hyperbolic groups and tessellations.

In philosophy, Lobachevsky changed our understanding of:

  • what mathematical truth is
  • how concepts can be independent of physical intuition
  • how axioms define worlds rather than describe them

He took a 2,000-year-old assumption and asked, “What if…?”And by answering that question, he opened the door to modern mathematics.

Conclusion

Nikolai Lobachevsky was not just a great mathematician — he was a conceptual pioneer. Working in isolation, lacking recognition, and facing skepticism from his peers, he nevertheless produced a new geometry that redefined the nature of mathematical thought.

He is the quintessential example of a scientist ahead of his time — misunderstood in life, celebrated in posterity, and foundational to how we understand space and logic today.

The Chebyshev Era and the Rise of the St. Petersburg School

With Pafnuty Chebyshev, Russian mathematics entered its first golden age. Active in the mid-19th century, Chebyshev:

  • founded the Petersburg mathematical tradition
  • produced groundbreaking work in number theory, probability, and approximation
  • trained a brilliant cohort: Korkin, Zolotarev, Markov, Voronoy, Lyapunov, Steklov

Although Steklov is the best remembered institutionally (the Steklov Institute bears his name), Chebyshev’s entire circle formed one of the world’s strongest mathematical communities. Their philosophy, inherited from Euler, was clear:start from concrete, practical problems, and develop new mathematics to solve them.

Moscow: A Later Bloom

Compared to St. Petersburg, Moscow lagged in mathematics until the early 20th century.

Dmitry Egorov

The first to bring serious mathematical research to Moscow University.

Nikolai Luzin

The true founder of the Moscow school. After studying in Paris under Lebesgue, Borel, and Baire, he brought back the new French ideas in set theory and measure theory — highly abstract compared to the Petersburg tradition.

Luzin’s students formed the core of the modern Moscow school:

  • Pavel Alexandrov
  • Andrey Kolmogorov
  • Dmitry Menshov
  • and many others

This lineage would eventually produce some of the 20th century’s greatest mathematicians.

Conclusion: How a Mathematical Civilization Was Built

Russian mathematics did not grow gradually from folk traditions or medieval scholarship. It began abruptly, almost artificially:

  • through Peter the Great’s political will
  • through the importation of Europe’s finest mathematicians
  • through Euler’s astonishing productivity and teaching
  • through the later flowering of native talent in St. Petersburg and Moscow

From these intertwined threads emerged one of the world’s richest mathematical cultures — a tradition that would, by the 20th century, rival and surpass its European origins.
