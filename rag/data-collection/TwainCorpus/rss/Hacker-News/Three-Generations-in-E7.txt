Three Generations in E7

It’s long been a mystery why there are 3 generations of quarks and leptons: three sets of particles, apparently identical except for how they interact with the Higgs boson.  It would be nice if there were some good physical explanation.  Nobody knows one.   Barring that, it would be nice if some beautiful mathematical structure made this pattern seem natural.   That’s what my new paper is about.

It’s my third paper about exceptional algebraic structures and the Standard Model.  When you classify famous gadgets in algebra, beautiful gadgets with fancy names like ‘simple Lie algebras’ and ‘Euclidean Jordan algebras’ and ‘positive hermitian Jordan pairs’, you tend to get infinite series of them—together with a few exceptions that can be built using the octonions.  This is a bit spooky, so I’ve been interested in this for a long time.

A few physicists have hoped that these exceptions are good for something.  For example, maybe the quirky features of our best theory of particle physics, the Standard Model, aren’t accidental.  Perhaps they fall out naturally from some exceptional algebraic structure.

It’s a long shot, but we’ve been stuck on figuring out new fundamental laws of particle physics for so long—roughly since the early 1980s—that it’s worth a try.

In 2018, 
Michel Dubois-Violette and Ivan Todorov
 noticed that the gauge group of the Standard Model falls out as symmetries of the so-called ‘exceptional Jordan algebra’ together with some ordinary Jordan algebras sitting inside it.  I tried to clarify that here, with a huge amount of help from an excellent young mathematician:

• John Baez and Paul Schwahn, 
The Standard Model gauge group from the exceptional Jordan algebra
.   (Blog article 
here
.)

It’s very nice, because the Jordan algebras in question arise naturally when you try to axiomatize the foundations of quantum physics.  It would be so cool if something about quantum physics made the Standard Model seem mathematically natural!

But really this result only concerns the gauge bosons in the Standard Model: the photon, gluons, and the W and Z bosons.  It says nothing about the fermions—that is, the quarks and leptons.  And it seems quite hard to get those into the picture.

In 2020, 
Latham Boyle
 tried to solve this problem by tensoring the exceptional Jordan algebra with the complex numbers.  This made 
one generation
 of fermions appear quite naturally!  But the connection to the foundations of quantum physics seemed lost: tensoring the exceptional Jordan algebra with the complex numbers seems at first like it might be just a formal trick.

This spring, Latham and his student Endre Bokor and I showed the connection to quantum physics is 
not
 lost:

• John Baez, Endre Bokor and Latham Boyle,

.  (Blog article 
here

The idea is to work, not with Jordan algebras, but with more general things called Jordan pairs, which have been studied by mathematicians since at least 1975.  We showed that you can still do quantum physics with Jordan pairs.  And we showed that there’s an ‘exceptional’ Jordan pair that naturally contains the Standard Model gauge group and one generation of fermions!

Since the rise of large language models the inflow has increased: I get about two emails a day from someone telling me they’ve made a revolutionary discovery in physics.  Writing the paper

For several weeks I checked, reorganized, expanded and completely rewrote this material. I avoid flying to conferences, which in some ways cripples my ability to keep up with new trends and influence people—but I don’t mind that.  It gives me more time to think.

I will think carefully about my next move.

Mitchell, I don’t want to hijack John’s post, but the most satisfying answers to these questions I found are in this paper, Lie Group Cosmology:

https://arxiv.org/abs/1506.08073

To summarize: one starts with a big exceptional Lie group (which includes the algebraic structure of spinors) and allows this Lie group to deform over a 4D subspace, described by a dynamic Maurer-Cartan connection 1-form with nonvanishing curvature, governed by a generalized Yang-Mills action over the deforming Lie group manifold. Fermions are Grassmann because they are 1-form parts of the connection orthogonal to the embedded 4D spacetime. Everything comes out of this picture — spin connection, frame, Higgs, gauge fields, fermions, and their dynamics.

Mitchell wrote:

  I can’t think of a single work in this genre that explains why the variables that are nominally “fermions” or “gauge bosons”, actually have those properties.

Typically you start with a Lie group called the gauge group, write down a Yang-Mills theory with this gauge group, and get one gauge boson for each generator of its Lie algebra; these are spin-1 particles.  Then you take a representation of this group on the fermions of your theory; these are spin-1/2 particles.  Then you take a representation on the Higgs boson; these are spin-1 particles.  There is little or no known room to deviate from this prescription if you’re seeking a perturbatively renormalizable quantum field theory.  There are theorems about this, which I have not taken the time to study, and which may admit loopholes.

  On the other hand, this theoretical genre is almost defined by the method of starting with a big group, finding standard model multiplets inside it, and then leaving issues of spin to be resolved in an unknown way at a future time….

It depends what you mean by “this genre”.   Physicists usually try to formulate theories of physics, and an adequate theory of physics needs to specify the spins of particles and a whole lot more (the Lagrangian).  This makes perfect sense, since their goal is to make physical predictions, and you can’t make physical predictions without specifying a lot of this stuff.

But maybe you’re talking about the much smaller, nay microscopic genre that I’m working in. I’m not formulating a theory of physics and I’m not trying to make physical predictions.  I’m just examining exceptional algebraic structures that show signs of similarity to the Standard Model, proving theorems about math.  I’m doing this in hopes that eventually this will shed some light on things—things like why the Standard Model is what it is, or how to resolve various questions that the Standard Model may not answer.

So yes, in this tiny genre there are papers that don’t discuss spin, in order to focus on the Standard Model gauge group and its representation on fermions.  But bringing spin into the game is a clear next step, and that’s what I’m thinking about now.

This is a consistent possibility, but the problem with this attitude is that if everyone has it, and there 
is
 something mathematically special about the laws we see, we many never find that.  Tons of smart physicists are investigating the multiverse.  So I decided, as a high-risk, high-reward activity, to keep seeking elegant mathematical patterns connected to the Standard Model.  As long as these take the form of theorems, they can’t be a 
complete
 waste of time.

I’m skeptical of this as an explanation of why Kepler’s polyhedral hypothesis lost its appeal. The hypothesis for Kepler did not have the character of his first two laws. Rather, it spoke to God’s architectural plan for the universe. (Incidentally, the same holds for his third “law”.)

Bear in mind also that Kepler explained the first two laws as a consequence of 
quasi-magnetic forces
. His physics was wrong, but it still fits into the “initial condition plus evolution” paradigm as much as the Newtonian version.

From what I’ve read, Kepler’s polyhedral hypothesis never found widespread acceptance. Initially the same was true for his first two laws (especially the second), but the dramatic success of the Rudolphine tables helped dispel that. In contrast, Kepler had to indulge in some fudging to get the polyhedral hypothesis to work at all. As astronomical distances became more precisely known, the polyhedral hypothesis would have looked worse and worse.
