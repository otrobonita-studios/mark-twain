“No published policy found” is four different situations

39 of the 152 country cells resolve to unknown. Reviewing them together showed they were not one state but four, and the difference changes what you should do next — so every unknown cell now carries a second field naming which one it is, and a citation recording where we looked. “We could not find it” is only useful to you if you can see where we searched.

—
No policy published
The provider publishes no country-of-residence policy anywhere we looked. The citation on this cell is the page such a policy would live on.
—
Named, but not as residency policy
Provider material names this country — as a wire destination, for spend-management support, in marketing, or as a country of incorporation. None of those is evidence about founder residency.
—
Absent from a non-exhaustive list
The provider publishes a supported-country list that is explicitly non-exhaustive, so this country’s absence from it carries no information in either direction.
—
Provider pages conflict
Two live provider pages disagree and the provider publishes no single verdict. This is an unresolved conflict, not an absence of information.

“Explicitly restricted” is three different situations

61 cells resolve to restricted, and only 9 of them name the country on a published prohibited list. In 40, the provider names no country anywhere — the bar comes from a blanket rule such as “US citizens or permanent residents only”. The answer you get is the same in both cases, and it is correct in both. What differs is how much is published about you specifically, which is what you need if you plan to ask the provider or appeal. So each restricted cell now names its evidence type too.

✕
Named on a prohibited list
The provider publishes a prohibited or unsupported country list and this country appears on it by name. This is the strongest form of restriction evidence in the index.
✕
Absent from a closed eligibility list
The provider publishes an eligibility list that its own wording makes exhaustive — residence in a listed country is stated as a requirement, not an example. Absence from a closed list is a deduction from what the provider stated, not an inference from silence.
✕
Derived from a blanket rule
The provider names no countries anywhere. The restriction follows from a blanket rule — US citizens or permanent residents only, or foreign business customers cannot apply. The conclusion holds for the reader, but no country-level source exists behind this cell.

  • The prohibited-countries list is set by Relay's banking partner and displays no publication or update date; it can change without notice.
  • The list runs to 31 countries of citizenship or residency and reaches well past the eight columned in this index. A reader whose country has no column here learns nothing from that absence — read the linked list. Named on it and not columned: Venezuela, Cambodia, Ethiopia, Haiti, Lebanon, Nicaragua, Ukraine and Bosnia and Herzegovina, among others (verified against the list 2026-07-15).
  • Relay's help center states each beneficial owner's personal address is a physical US address, while also stating it accepts owners based outside the US; Relay's published pages do not reconcile the two statements.
  • The remote-onboarding description comes from a provider marketing blog post, not the help center; no in-person or video-KYC requirement is published either way.
  • ITIN is not mentioned on Relay's published pages; the stated identifiers are SSN or passport number.
  • Acceptance is not the end of the question at Relay, and the constraint is one-directional. No page states which applicants land in Basic, nor how to leave it. The agreement directs the customer to log in to see their own limits — so this cell cannot be resolved from published sources for any specific applicant.

How this index is built

Every claim starts as a verbatim quote from a fetchable source — the provider's own help center, terms of service, or published country list (tier T1), an official provider blog or support statement (T2), or a dated user report (T3, labeled as such). A separate adversarial verification pass then re-fetched every source and discarded any claim whose quote was absent, misread its context, or overreached — 239 of 268 mined claims survived. Country cells are deliberately tri-state: a country absent from a provider's restricted list is reported as “no published restriction,” never converted into “accepted.”

Rows marked †(receiving from abroad, card issuance, phone & 2FA) were added after that pass by single-pass primary-source research — same quote-and-cite standard, but not yet through the adversarial re-verification the core went through, so they are excluded from the verified-claim count until the next quarterly release re-verifies them.

Provider policies change without notice, and application outcomes also depend on provider-side review of each business. This index documents what providers publish, as of the verification date — it is a map of published policy, not a prediction of any individual application outcome. Source pages are snapshotted to the Internet Archive at verification time. Full methodology: evidence basis.
