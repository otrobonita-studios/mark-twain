Method

Every number on this page is measured, with one exception.

    Nothing here is a forecast or a projection. The exception is 
What tariffs cost
    your household
, above, which needs an assumption no dataset can supply. It says
    so at the top of the section and carries a label wherever it appears. Elsewhere, when
    a question could only be answered by assuming something, we say so and leave the
    number out.

Two tariffs, two payers.
 Canada's counter-tariffs are paid by Canadian
    importers and show up in Canadian prices. What they hit on this side is American
    exporters, the people who make what is on the list. Washington's tariffs on Canadian
    goods are paid at the American border by American importers, and that is the duty
    counted in 
What you have already paid
. Two different taxes with two different
    payers. Do not add them together.

What this page does not tell you
, because the data cannot:

How much of the duty on 
Canadian
 goods reaches a shelf price. That is
      pass-through, and it is a margin decision made firm by firm. The Canada figures above
      assume nothing about it. They are duty collected at the border. The one modelled
      number on this page does state a pass-through rate, but for tariffs on all U.S.
      imports rather than Canada's alone. See below.

What a tariff will do to any future price, at the pump or anywhere else.

How many jobs are at risk. Turning export dollars into a job count needs an
      attribution model we do not have.

What share of any industry's exports is tariffed. The tariff list is classified by
      HS and the industry data by NAICS; joining them takes a concordance we do not have,
      and guessing one would manufacture a number.

    Counting tariff lines tells you how wide the list is, not how much money is on it. One
    line can carry a billion dollars of trade or almost none. That is why the dollar figures
    come from trade data instead of from the list.
